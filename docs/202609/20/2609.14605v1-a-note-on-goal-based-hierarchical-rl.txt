# A note on goal-based hierarchical RL 

Kevin Murphy September 15, 2026 

Abstract 

The agent-centric general value function (ACGVF) construction of Nangue Tasse et al. [2026] lets the agent make two decisions that are normally imposed by the environment or agent designer: which goal to pursue and when to declare a goal as finished (in addition to choosing the action). This is a very general framework that subsumes almost all prior work on reinforcement learning, control and planning, as well as more general formalisms proposed in the cognitive sciences. However, it assumes the environment is fully observed, i.e., that the observation is a sufficient statistic. In Murphy [2025], a general agent design was proposed where the policy is based on an internal belief state zt and an internal goal; however, the goals were assumed to be externally provided. In this note, we unify and extend these two approaches using the formalism of hierarchical hidden Markov models (HHMM) [Murphy and Paskin, 2001]. 

# 1 Introduction 

The agent-centric general value function (ACGVF) construction of Nangue Tasse et al. [2026] starts with a fully observed MDP and augments the agent’s decision with a goal Gt ∈ G and a Boolean done action Dt ∈ { 0, 1}. Its key conceptual step is that goals and termination are selected by the agent in the service of environmental reward, rather than specified as part of the task. However, it assumes the world state St is used directly to define the policy π(g, a, d | s). Thus St is implicitly both the Markov state of the world and the sufficient information state of the controller. The generic agent architecture in Murphy [2025] separates these two objects, and uses a policy of the form πθ(a | z, g ), where g is the goal and Zt is the internal memory state updated according to 

Zt+1 = Uϕ(Zt, A t, O t+1 ). (1) The policy therefore acts on the history summary Zt, not directly on the observation Ot. However, it treats g as an external input. In this note, we propose to combine these approaches by making the goal another component of the agent’s dynamical state. There are now two internal timescales: 1. the goal state Gt, which is piecewise constant; and 2. the within-goal state Zt, which is updated after every observation. The done action is the boundary event coupling them. # 5 Learning and inference 

There are two interpretations of the hidden nodes, leading to different algorithms. 

Goals are latent explanations. If Gt and Dt are not recorded—for example, when discovering goals from demonstrations—then equation (3) is a controlled HHMM likelihood. In this case we can estimate parameters using EM. The DBN construction of Murphy and Paskin [2001] gives an algorithm for an exact E step for discrete states that is linear in sequence length for fixed hierarchy depth and state cardinality. Amortized filtering, particle methods, or structured variational inference are natural for neural and continuous Zt. The persistence constraint in equation (4) prevents the goal label from changing arbitrarily at every step and therefore acts as a useful segmentation prior. 

Goals are internal actions. If the agent chooses its own values for Gt and Dt, then these variables are observed from the agent’s perspective even though they are hidden from the environment. One can learn: 

• Uϕ jointly with the policies using the actor–critic gradients described below; 

• Qg or Wg using TD learning on tuples ( zt, g t, a t, d t, r t+1 , o t+1 , z t+1 ); 

• πθ(a, d | z, g ) using value-based or actor–critic updates; and 5• μη(g | z) using only boundary transitions, with the duration-aware return in equation (13). The single primitive-time replay stream can train both levels; boundaries provide the segmentation automatically. We give more details below.

In summary, the HHMM framing brings the following benefits: 1. Temporal commitment. The HHMM gate turns “choose a goal” into a persistent intention rather than an independent per-step label. 2. Partial observability. Goal choice, action choice, and termination depend on the agent’s filtered state Zt, removing the need to identify observation with world state. 3. Call–return semantics. The done action becomes an explicit return from the within-goal controller to the goal selector. 4. Two valid objectives. Segment-level ACGVFs and continual hierarchical values differ exactly by the upper-level continuation term in equation (11). 5. Structured credit assignment. Primitive rewards train the lower controller, while boundary-to-boundary returns train the goal selector. 6. A route to deeper hierarchies. Additional states Q1 

> t

, . . . , Q Lt and finish variables F 1 

> t

, . . . , F Lt

represent nested intentions. A level may change only when all descendants below it have returned, retaining the proper nesting constraint of an HHMM. In short, the ACGVF proposal supplies the missing agency—goals and completion are chosen internally—while the recurrent-state agent supplies the missing information-state machinery. The HHMM supplies the missing temporal grammar: select a goal, call a goal-conditioned controller, update internal state from observations while the goal remains active, and return control upward when the agent declares completion.
