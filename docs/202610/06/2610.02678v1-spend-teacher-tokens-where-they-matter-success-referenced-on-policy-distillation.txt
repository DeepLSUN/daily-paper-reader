## ABSTRACT 

On-policy distillation (OPD) combines student-generated rollouts with dense token-level supervision from a teacher, but providing such supervision for ev-ery rollout requires substantial teacher computation. We introduce Success-Referenced On-Policy Distillation (SR-OPD), which reduces this cost by selecting which prompts and rollouts receive teacher supervision. When the student pro-duces both successful and failed rollouts for the same prompt, a successful rollout can serve as a natural reference for selecting failed rollouts. SR-OPD therefore focuses on such prompts and prioritizes failed rollouts whose hidden-state trajec-tories show sustained divergence from a successful reference, while accounting for estimated teacher-input cost. Across three teacher–student pairs and six math-ematical reasoning benchmarks, SR-OPD uses only 3.46–5.02% of the teacher-input tokens required by Vanilla OPD in the one-pass setting while maintaining comparable reasoning performance. Under a controlled setting matched to 5% of Vanilla OPD’s teacher-input budget, further experiments support both key design choices: focusing supervision on prompts with both successful and failed rollouts, and using successful rollouts to guide failure selection. These results indicate that a student’s own successful behavior can serve as a useful reference for allocat-ing teacher supervision under a fixed teacher-input budget. Code is available at 

https://github.com/ChenXiangYpily1234/SR-OPD .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on rollouts generated by its current policy using su-pervision from a teacher model. By aligning training with the student’s inference-time rollout dis-tribution, OPD mitigates the train–test mismatch of offline distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). However, querying the teacher for every rollout incurs substantial cost. Such uniform supervision can be inefficient because the value of teacher supervision varies across prompts, roll-outs, and token positions, making uniform allocation of teacher computation undesirable under a fixed teacher-input budget. This raises a natural question: can the student decide, before teacher inference, which prompts and rollouts should receive teacher supervision? Recent work improves OPD efficiency through selective supervision. Methods such as TIP (Xu et al., 2026), TA-OPD (Wang et al., 2026), and SelecTKD (Huang et al., 2025) identify or reweight informative token-level supervision, improving the use of teacher signals within student rollouts. However, token-level selection does not by itself avoid the teacher-input cost of processing an entire queried rollout. A closely related direction is TLR (Anonymous, 2026), which performs pre-query rollout routing in OPSD, where supervision is provided by a privileged-conditioned version of the same model rather than a separate teacher model. Thus, pre-query routing for standard teacher– student OPD remains underexplored. To address this gap, we introduce Success-Referenced On-Policy Distillation (SR-OPD) , a two-stage pre-query routing method illustrated in Figure 1. A similar filtering principle has been used in on-policy reinforcement learning, for example in DAPO’s dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025). In 1arXiv:2610.02678v1 [cs.AI] 2 Oct 2026 SR-OPD, however, this filtering serves a different purpose: prompts for which the student produces both successful and failed rollouts provide failed rollouts to supervise and a same-prompt successful rollout that can serve as a teacher-free reference. The first stage, prompt routing , therefore retains such prompts while skipping all-success prompts, which contain no failed rollout to target, and all-failure prompts, which lack a successful reference for the second stage. Supervision signals in on-policy distillation. Recent work has explored how to identify where teacher supervision is most useful. TA-OPD (Wang et al., 2026) focuses on the teachability of token-level teacher signals, while PG-OPD (Zhao et al., 2026) uses teacher–student compatibility over short prefixes to estimate rollout value. These studies show that teacher or teacher–student in-formation can guide supervision allocation. However, obtaining such information may itself require teacher computation. This raises a complementary question: can supervision value be estimated using only information already available from the student’s own rollouts? 

Same-prompt outcome signals. When multiple rollouts are generated for the same prompt, their outcomes provide direct student-side information about what the student can solve and where it still fails. Recent distillation studies provide evidence for the value of such signals. ReNIO (Lin et al., 2026) shows that incorrect rollouts can provide stronger learning signals than correct-only rollouts, while MOPD (Yu et al., 2026) shows that successful and failed same-prompt rollouts provide com-plementary information for teacher supervision. Together, these findings suggest that both failure information and the contrast between successful and failed behavior can help identify where super-vision is valuable. However, outcome labels remain coarse: multiple failed rollouts from the same prompt share the same failure label even though their reasoning processes may differ substantially. 

Hidden-state signals for failure selection. This limitation motivates finer-grained signals for dis-tinguishing among failed rollouts. Hidden-state trajectories provide such information by revealing how internal representations evolve throughout reasoning. Sun et al. (2026), Liang et al. (2026), and Chen et al. (2026) identify representational differences between correct and incorrect reasoning and use hidden states for verification, candidate selection, or fine-grained credit assignment. These findings motivate using representation geometry as a teacher-free signal for distinguishing among failed rollouts beyond what outcome labels alone can provide. 

## 3 METHOD 

Motivated by evidence that successful and failed rollouts from the same prompt provide comple-mentary information for teacher supervision, we ask whether the student’s own successful behavior can be used before teacher inference to decide where expensive supervision should be allocated. In SR-OPD, successful rollouts are not sent to the teacher; instead, they serve as teacher-free references for routing. This perspective focuses attention on mixed-outcome prompts: their failed rollouts pro-vide candidates for supervision, while their successful rollouts provide same-prompt references for deciding which failure to query. Based on this principle, SR-OPD introduces a two-stage pre-query routing procedure before stan-dard OPD supervision. For each training prompt, the student first generates multiple rollouts. The first stage, prompt routing, determines whether the prompt should receive a teacher query. For each retained prompt, the second stage, success-referenced rollout routing, compares failed rollouts against same-prompt successful behavior and selects one failure using persistent representational departure together with estimated teacher-input cost. Both routing decisions rely only on student-side information and are made before teacher inference. The selected failed rollout then receives the unchanged standard OPD supervision target. We first define the routing setup in Section 3.1, then present prompt routing in Section 3.2 and rollout routing in Section 3.3. 3.1 ROUTING SETUP 

We first formalize the student-side information available before teacher inference and the candidate sets used by the two routing stages. In OPD, we use x to denote a training prompt. For each x, the student generates K rollouts, 

G(x) = {y(1) , . . . , y (K)}. (1) A task verifier assigns each rollout an outcome 

v(x, y ) ∈ { 0, 1, ⊥} , (2) 3where 1 denotes success, 0 denotes failure, and ⊥ denotes an invalid or unevaluable rollout. Rollouts with v(x, y ) = ⊥ are discarded before routing. The remaining rollouts are divided into successful and failed sets, 

Px = {y ∈ G (x) : v(x, y ) = 1 }, Nx = {y ∈ G (x) : v(x, y ) = 0 }. (3) Here, Px contains the successful rollouts available as same-prompt references, while Nx contains the failed candidates considered for teacher supervision. Routing changes only which student rollout receives teacher supervision; the OPD objective applied after selection remains unchanged and is given in Appendix A.1. 3.2 PROMPT ROUTING 

Given the successful and failed sets Px and Nx, the first stage decides whether prompt x is eligible for a teacher query. SR-OPD retains a prompt only when both sets are nonempty: 

q(x) = 1 [Px̸ = ∅ ∧ N x̸ = ∅] . (4) When q(x) = 0 , no teacher query is issued. An all-success prompt contains no failed candidate to supervise, whereas an all-failure prompt lacks the same-prompt successful reference required by the second stage. When q(x) = 1 , Nx provides the failed candidates and Px provides the successful references generated by the current student, so the prompt proceeds to rollout routing. This gate does not assume that skipped prompts have no learning value; it only restricts teacher queries to prompts for which the proposed second-stage routing rule is defined. For a retained prompt, the remaining question is which failed candidate in Nx should receive the teacher query. 3.3 SUCCESS -R EFERENCED ROLLOUT ROUTING 

For a routed prompt, all candidates in Nx share the same failure outcome, so the binary verifier la-bel cannot distinguish which failure should receive teacher supervision. We therefore use successful rollouts in Px as student-side references and compare each failed rollout with successful behavior already produced for the same prompt. This provides a finer-grained teacher-free signal for dis-tinguishing among failures. The selector is constructed in two steps: we first quantify persistent representational departure from successful references, and then combine this signal with estimated teacher-input cost.

## 5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Discussion. The results suggest that teacher-supervision allocation can be decomposed into two distinct decisions: which prompts should trigger a teacher query, and which failed rollout within those prompts should receive supervision. This hierarchical view reframes teacher supervision from uniform processing of student rollouts into a pre-query allocation problem, and motivates the sepa-rate controlled analyses of prompt routing and rollout routing. The stage-level experiments provide the clearest evidence for these two routing decisions, while the component ablations and teacher-side diagnostics are better viewed as complementary mechanism evidence rather than as independently resolved performance gains. This framework also suggests two natural directions for future work. First, this study focuses pri-marily on pre-query rollout selection, which reduces the number of rollouts sent to the teacher before teacher inference. A complementary direction is post-query token selection, which determines how to use teacher information after it has already been obtained. Future work could study their joint optimization, with the goal of reducing the number of teacher queries while also improving the utilization of the supervision returned by each query, thereby further improving the performance– efficiency trade-off of OPD. Second, the rollout-selection mechanism studied here is based on comparing multiple student-generated rollouts for the same prompt. This idea is not inherently restricted to teacher–student OPD. An important extension is to examine whether success-referenced selection can be incorpo-rated into multi-rollout training paradigms such as GRPO, where it may serve as a more general trajectory-selection mechanism for identifying training rollouts that are more useful among multiple candidates. 8Limitations. Teacher cost counts serialized teacher-input tokens, excluding student generation, verification, hidden-state routing, and optimization; the savings do not establish equivalent reduc-tions in total compute or wall-clock time. The controlled studies match teacher-input use, not prompt exposure or optimizer updates. With three paired training seeds, component effects remain direc-tional rather than independently resolved. SR-OPD requires reliable verifier outcomes and mixed-outcome prompts; its success reference is unavailable for all-success or all-failure prompts. Evaluation covers mathematical reasoning, three teacher–student pairs, and a limited range of rollout counts, so generalization remains open. Rollout-count sensitivity and verifier robustness are reported in Appendices A.10 and A.11. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Success-Referenced On-Policy Distillation (SR-OPD), a two-stage pre-query routing method for standard teacher–student OPD. SR-OPD reduces unnecessary teacher queries by making both prompt-level and rollout-level routing decisions before teacher inference. Its central idea is to use successful behavior already produced by the student as a reference for allocating teacher supervision, while leaving the standard OPD supervision objective unchanged. Across three teacher–student pairs and six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, SR-OPD uses only 3.46–5.02% of the teacher-input tokens required by Vanilla OPD in the one-pass setting while main-taining comparable reasoning performance. Controlled experiments under a matched teacher-input budget further support both prompt routing and success-referenced rollout selection. These results show that student-generated success can serve not only as an outcome of training, but also as an internal reference signal for allocating teacher supervision. More broadly, they sug-gest that expensive teacher computation in OPD need not be distributed uniformly across student-generated rollouts, but can instead be routed using student-side information available before teacher inference.
