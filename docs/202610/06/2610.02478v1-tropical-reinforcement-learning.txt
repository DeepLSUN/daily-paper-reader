# TROPICAL REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

Arip Asadulaev † Aladin Djuhera ‡ Karim Salta † Holger Boche ‡ Fakhri Karray † Martin Takac †

## ABSTRACT 

Reinforcement learning for large language models typically maximizes expected return, adding up the probabilities of all successful trajectories. However, the classical sum formulation can only report how often the model policy succeeds, not which solution actually worked , and because probabilities sum to one, reinforcing one solution can make the model forget another that was never shown to be wrong. This makes expected return a poor fit for compositional reasoning, where a solution must be assembled from reasoning steps that the model produces in separate, often failed, attempts but rarely produces together. To address this, we propose Tropical Reinforcement Learning, which rests on a simple change of algebra: instead of adding the probabilities of alternative solutions, we take their maximum, which yields the tropical semiring . The value of a state then becomes the log-probability of its most likely verified solution, together with an explicit path that can be replayed and reused. This enables true composition, since the best prefix and the best suffix meeting at a shared state can be joined even when they come from different rollouts. To put this into practice, we introduce T ROPIC , a training algorithm for deterministic, resettable environments with verifiable outcomes. On four agentic tasks (Sokoban, Countdown, FrozenLake, WebShop), T ROPIC outperforms the strongest on-policy baselines by up to 16 percentage points. Changing the algebra of reinforcement learning, not just its estimators, can thus substantially improve compositional reasoning in language models. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Figure 1: Sokoban pass@k results 

The best solution a language model can produce is often one it has never produced. A model may know how to formalize a problem, derive an intermediate result, invoke the right theorem, and check an an-swer, and may have done each of these in earlier attempts, yet never have chained them in the order a new problem requires (Press et al., 2023; Lake & Ba-roni, 2018). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is the standard post-training tool for closing this gap by sampling many attempts and rein-forcing the ones a verifier accepts (Kimi Team et al., 2025; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026; GLM-5-Team et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Wen et al., 2026). However, the classical expected-return formulation learns only from what it samples, as a trajectory is credited only after the policy has generated it end-to-end. Recent works therefore focus on trajectory design, improving which rollouts are sampled and how they are credited (Wang et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Yet these methods keep the expected-return objective, and with it the requirement that a solution be sampled in one piece. For composition, this requirement is the bottleneck: a solution that chains d fragments, each found in a separate attempt, is sampled in one piece with probability exponentially small in d, even though the model has already produced every part of it. Beyond failing to reward composition, expected return can also actively suppress the fragments it requires. Suppose the model produces outcomes A, B, their composition A ◦ B, and a failure 0, with probabilities (pA, p B , p AB , p 0). In general, a high expected reward bounds the total probability of currently successful behavior, but places no lower bound on any individual fragment that a future composition requires. This is consistent with recent findings that loss of policy entropy and reasoning diversity limits what RL post-training can achieve (Cui et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026). Both problems stem from the same choice: expected return adds up the probabilities of alternative trajectories. A sum does not record which trajectory succeeded, so fragments from different rollouts cannot be combined, and, because probabilities sum to one, raising one term lowers another. Tropical RL changes this algebra. It still adds log-probabilities along each trajectory, but combines alternative trajectories by their maximum , which yields the max-plus, or tropical , semiring. The value of a state becomes the log-probability of its most likely verified path, and, because addition distributes over the maximum, it splits at any shared state into a best prefix and a best suffix, possibly from different rollouts. Compositions can thus be assembled without ever being sampled in one piece, and since the maximum ignores all but the best path, the value never loses a verified path once found. RL thus shifts from learning what the policy samples to learning what can be assembled from it. We develop this shift from first principles to a practical algorithm. Our contributions are: (i) a

Compositional reasoning and generalization. We call a solution compositional when it is a sequence of reusable computations, τ = f1 ◦ · · · ◦ fd, in which each fragment fi, such as a reduction, a theorem application, or an arithmetic step, starts at the state produced by the previous one. Compositional reasoning is the ability to produce such a sequence for a given problem. Compositional generalization 

is the ability to solve problems whose solutions combine known fragments in a way that has not been observed before (Lake & Baroni, 2018; Keysers et al., 2020; Press et al., 2023). In this paper, “not observed” has a specific meaning: every fragment of the solution may already have appeared in some rollout, but never all of them together in a single one. The central difference between the two algebras shows up when the actor is trained. Under expected return, reinforcing one solution takes probability from the others, and a verified solution can lose value without ever being refuted (Proposition 1). Under the maximum, the opposite holds.

The state s in (ii) need not lie on τ ⋆ and may never have been trained from. Its best solution can be a prefix from one rollout joined at u to a suffix trained as part of another, and it inherits the full gain made on that suffix. The reason is a property of the maximum: the step can lower the probability of actions that are not on the best path, but no best path uses them, so no value sees the loss. Under a sum every alternative counts, and the loss is always seen. The theorem is exact for an actor with separate parameters per state. With shared parameters, as in a language model, a step on τ ⋆ also moves the actor at other states, and the guarantee holds up to this interference.

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Recent work shows that RL post-training can teach language models to compose skills they already have (Yuan et al., 2026; Abdulsalam et al., 2026; Fu & Liu, 2026; Kong et al., 2026) and can improve reasoning beyond the base model (Wen et al., 2026), although its gains may stay within the base model’s sampling support (Yue et al., 2025). Many methods improve RL training for LLMs: DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) and SNR-aware filtering (Wang et al., 2026) address training collapse, GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) and VinePPO (Kazemnejad et al., 2024) refine step-level credit, process supervision rewards intermediate steps (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024), and resets have been used for credit assignment (Samanta et al., 2026). All of these works optimize expected return, so a composition must be sampled in one piece before it is reinforced. They are complementary to ours, which changes how alternative trajectories are aggregated. Other works move beyond expected return. Expected return sums the probabilities of verified paths, MaxRL takes the logarithm of this sum, and Tropical RL takes its maximum, the zero-temperature limit in which alternatives are selected rather than summed. Closer to our method, tree search retains and extends promising partial trajectories (Yao et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2024), T-STAR (Li et al., 2026) grafts corrected steps onto shared trajectory structure, and Liu et al. (2026) preserve correct prefixes when penalizing errors. These methods reuse trajectories that were sampled; T ROPIC also composes verified solutions that were never sampled in one piece. We compare against T-STAR in Section 6.1. Finally, max-plus methods have been used to compute or approximate value functions of known deterministic control problems (Akian et al., 2008; McEneaney, 2006; Berthier & Bach, 2020; Gonçalves, 2021; Bach, 2019). We instead use the max-plus semiring to define the learning objective of a language-model policy. 

## 6 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

Environments. We evaluate T ROPIC on four interactive tasks from RAGEN-2 (Wang et al., 2026): (i) Sokoban (Weber et al., 2018), a grid-based planning task in which the agent pushes boxes onto targets while avoiding irreversible dead ends so that solutions depend on reusing good partial prefixes, (ii) Countdown (Katz et al., 2026), an exact-target arithmetic task with many valid solutions in which intermediate results from different attempts can be recombined, (iii) FrozenLake (Brockman et al., 2016), a grid world with terminal holes and sparse rewards in which safe partial paths must be retained and extended, and (iv) WebShop (Yao et al., 2023a), a long-horizon web-shopping task in which search, navigation, and attribute selection must be composed under delayed feedback. All environments are deterministic and resettable. Further details are provided in App. B. 

Training and Baselines. For each task, we train Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025) and Gemma 4 E4B-it (Gemma Team, 2026) using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), RAGEN’s SNR-Aware method (Wang et al., 2026), and T ROPIC .All methods are trained under a matched experimental setup, using the same GPU, task-relevant observations, rollout budget, environment workers, and the same observation representation. We discuss the differences in restarts, replay, and stopping in App. C. We evaluate all methods across three independent training seeds on a predefined held-out split of unseen instances. We report the task success rate, end-to-end training time, and the average number of generated tokens per episode. 

Results. Table 2 and Fig. 4 show that T ROPIC achieves the highest success rate on every task and for both models, while generating a comparable or smaller number of tokens and training faster in all but one setting. ## 7 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

RL has moved from tabular dynamic programming to deep function approximation and now to post-training large language models. Each wave changed how values are represented and estimated, but all kept the same objective. This objective measures how often a sampled attempt succeeds. Compositional reasoning instead asks which verified solution can be assembled from what the model has already produced. We showed that answering this question requires only changing the viewpoint of the algebra and herewith a single operation. Alternative trajectories can be combined by a maximum instead of a sum. The resulting tropical value names a concrete verified path, never loses a path once found, and splits at any shared state into a best prefix and a best suffix, so solutions can be assembled from fragments that were never sampled together. Our concrete implementation TROPIC builds on these properties and outperforms the strongest on-policy baselines on every task and model we test. We believe this change matters most for self-improving models. In particular, such a model must keep what it has verified and build new solutions from old ones. Transferring this growing set fully into the model’s weights is the natural next step. Tropical RL asks what can be built from what the model has already found, and we believe this question will define the next wave. 9Preprint. Work in progress.

## A STRUCTURAL RESULTS AND PROOFS 

This appendix collects the structural results behind Sections 3–4.3 and proves them in full. The main text established three facts: expected return is not a sufficient statistic for the best witness (observation 1), a policy-indexed value can forget a verified computation, and the requirements of retention force the max–plus semiring (Proposition ?? ). Here we go further in four directions. We show that the three objectives in our related-work table are three distances from the actor to the set of certified computations, and that the tropical one is the only distance to a computation rather than to a distribution over computations (Section A.2); this is the result we regard as central, because it turns the choice of algebra into the choice of what the learner is being pulled toward. We show that the tropical learning signal is the unique one that depends only on which computations have been found and not on how often (Section A.3). We prove a policy improvement theorem in which training on a single certified path improves the tropical value at every state of the retained graph, with the improvement propagating undamped to interfaces that were never trained from, and we prove that this improvement step is not an expected-return improvement step and cannot be selected from expected-return information (Section A.4). Finally we prove the tropical analogue of the performance difference lemma, derive from it the tropical policy gradient and its lack of visitation weights, and give an exponential-versus-logarithmic separation between expected-return and tropical gradient ascent on a chain (Section A.5). One idea organizes all of it. The tropical value is a functional of a set of verified computations, aggregated by a maximum; expected return is a functional of a measure over them, aggregated by a sum. A set grows under discovery and has a most accessible element; a measure is normalized and has only a total. Every theorem below is a consequence of one of three properties of the maximum that the sum lacks: it is idempotent, it is attained by one of its arguments, and it is insensitive to what happens to its non-maximal arguments. Proposition 1 located the cause of forgetting in normalization: probability granted to one computation is taken from another. A related cause of collapse is frequency bias : in on-policy learning the weight a computation receives in the update is its sampling probability, so computations that are found often are reinforced more, are then found more often, and so on. One would like a learning signal that depends on which computations have been discovered but not on how many times . This section shows that this requirement is exactly idempotence of the aggregation, hence, by Theorem 6, exactly the maximum.

A.4 COMPOSITIONAL POLICY IMPROVEMENT 

The classical policy improvement theorem says that acting greedily with respect to an exact Qπ

produces a policy at least as good as π at every state. It requires Qπ (s, a ) for every action at every state visited, which for expected return means the total success mass of every subtree. Remark 2 (Where the separation lies) . On this chain there is a single certified path, so Jtrop =log JER exactly, and part (b) also describes gradient ascent on log JER , which is the maximum-likelihood objective of MaxRL. The separation in Theorem 15 is therefore between expected return and its logarithm, and the obstruction in (a) is the factor JER in the gradient, which is the weight 

ωER = Pθ (τ ) of Proposition 3. What distinguishes the tropical objective from maximum likelihood is not this rate but the object it moves toward, as established in Section A.2 and in Proposition 4: on a problem with several certified paths the maximum-likelihood gradient is spread over them by posterior weight, while the tropical gradient is the score function of one. The theorem is stated with exact gradients to make clear that the exponential cost in (a) is a property of the objective’s landscape, not of sampling; no critic, baseline or additional data removes it. With sampled gradients every expected-return method also pays ρ−d to observe the path at all, and that cost is the one the archive removes. 

A.6 HOW TROPIC REALIZES THESE RESULTS 

The algorithm of Section 4.3 is the loop these results describe. The archive is the set of certified vertices of Section A.2; discovery adds vertices and composition adds vertices assembled from existing ones, and by Theorem 3 the set never shrinks. Rescoring under the frozen actor recomputes the distances from the current actor to each vertex. Corollary 1(a) is why the prefix and suffix passes may be run separately and joined. The improvement step is maximum likelihood on that nearest vertex, which Corollary 1(b) identifies as the exact gradient of the tropical value, Corollary 2 identifies as maximizing a penalty-free lower bound on the true improvement, and Theorem 12 shows to be a pointwise improvement step on the whole retained graph. By Theorem 7 and Proposition 3 the step depends on the archive only through its support, which is why acquisition may be as off-policy as the frontier rule makes it, with no correction. By Proposition 4 it is not a step that expected-return information could have selected. What the actor is pulled toward, at every iteration and at every interface, is the nearest computation it has been shown to be capable of; what it is never pulled toward is a distribution over computations, and what it is never allowed to lose is a computation it has already been shown. 26 Preprint. As described in the main body, T ROPIC

Two attempts therefore share a state whenever they reach the same environment configuration at the same step, even if they got there by different actions. These shared states are the interfaces at which T ROPIC joins a prefix from one attempt with a suffix from another. For every task, we describe below what a state is, what the verifier checks, and why such interfaces arise. 

Sokoban. The agent moves on a 6 × 6 grid and must push a single box onto a target cell. A state is the board configuration, i.e., the positions of the agent and the box. An episode succeeds when the box rests on the target. Pushing the box against a wall or into a corner can make the puzzle unsolvable, so many attempts fail late after a sequence of correct early pushes. For expected-return methods, such attempts yield zero reward and their correct prefixes receive no credit. T ROPIC keeps these prefixes in its graph: if another attempt, started from the same board configuration, reaches the target, the two are joined into a verified solution that neither attempt produced alone. Because different move orders often lead to the same board, such shared configurations are frequent. 

Countdown. The agent is given three to six input numbers and a target, and must combine the numbers with +, −, ×, and ÷, using each number exactly once, to reach the target. A state is the multiset of numbers still available, and each step replaces two numbers by the result of an operation on them. The verifier checks that the final number equals the target. Because arithmetic steps on disjoint numbers commute, different attempts frequently reach the same intermediate multiset, e.g., 

{57 , 3} from 97 − 40 and 14 − 11 in either order. An attempt that computes a useful intermediate result but then fails, and another that finishes correctly from the same intermediate multiset, can therefore be joined into a verified derivation. Training and evaluation use disjoint problem splits. 

FrozenLake. The agent navigates a 4 × 4 grid from a start cell to a goal cell while avoiding holes, which end the episode. Each cell is safe with probability 0.9 when the map is generated, and transitions are deterministic in our experiments. A state is the agent’s position. Success requires an entire sequence of safe moves, and a single misstep anywhere ends the episode with zero reward, so failed attempts often contain long safe prefixes. T ROPIC retains these prefixes and restarts new attempts from the frontier of safe cells, so a safe route to the middle of the grid found in one attempt can be completed by a suffix found in another. 

WebShop. The agent receives a natural-language shopping instruction, e.g., a portable folding desk with a given finish below a price limit, and must search the catalog, navigate result and product pages, select the required attributes, and purchase a matching item. We use the RAGEN-2 

webshop-minimal adaptation. A state is the current page together with the selections made so far. An episode succeeds only if the purchased item satisfies all requested attributes. Episodes are long, 27 Preprint. Work in progress. and a mistake late in the episode, such as a wrong attribute, discards an otherwise correct search and navigation. T ROPIC keeps the correct search and navigation steps and can join them with a correct selection-and-purchase suffix found in another attempt from the same product page. 

Why these Tasks Favor Composition. The four environments share the structure that makes expected return inefficient and tropical composition effective: the reward is sparse and terminal, failures typically occur after many correct steps, and different attempts pass through the same intermediate states. On-policy baselines must sample a complete successful episode before any of its steps is reinforced. T ROPIC instead retains every valid step, including steps from failed attempts, and assembles verified solutions at shared states. This is the mechanism measured directly by FIRST -S OLVE in Sec. 6.1. 

## C TRAINING AND EVALUATION SETUP 

Our implementation builds on RAGEN-2 (Wang et al., 2026) and its VERL training backend (Sheng et al., 2025). We follow the default RAGEN-2 configuration where applicable and describe all task-and method-specific settings below. 

Models. We initialize all policies from Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025), with 3.1Bparameters, and Gemma-4-E4B-it (Gemma Team, 2026), with 4B effective and 8B total parameters including embeddings, i.e., more than twice as many parameters in total. The two models also differ in family, architecture, and pretraining data, which lets us test whether T ROPIC ’s gains depend on a particular model. All models are fully fine-tuned without parameter-efficient adapters. 

Baselines. We compare against PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and RAGEN-2’s SNR-Aware Filtering (Wang et al., 2026). All use AdamW with an actor learning rate of 10 −6, (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.999) , and weight decay 0.01 . PPO and SNR-Aware use a learned critic with learning rate 10 −5 and Generalized Advantage Estimation (Schulman et al., 2018) with γ = 1 and λGAE = 1 . GRPO and DAPO use group-relative advantages without a critic. For DAPO we use the standard implementation provided by RAGEN-2. The policy-ratio clipping interval is [0 .8, 1.28] , entropy regularization uses coefficient 0.001 , and no reference-policy KL penalty is applied. SNR-Aware Filtering applies the RAGEN-2 rollout-group filter with cumulative probability threshold p = 0 .9, using the within-group reward standard deviation as its selection score. The other baselines apply no rollout filtering. The additional methods in Table 3 (MaxRL, GiGPO, and GiGPO + T-STAR) use the same backbone, optimizer, and rollout budget, with the remaining hyperparameters set as recommended by their authors.

What the Evaluation Measures. We use compositional as described in the main body: a solution is compositional when its fragments have been produced in separate attempts but never together in a single one. T ROPIC targets exactly this situation during training, and F IRST -S OLVE measures it directly. On Sokoban, 31 .6% of solved training problems are first solved by a composition never sampled end to end. The held-out evaluation asks a separate question: whether the policy trained on these assembled solutions solves new instances on its own, in one attempt, without any of T ROPIC ’s search machinery. Since every held-out instance is unseen during training, success requires producing a new solution rather than recalling a stored one. On the Fairness of the Comparison. TROPIC differs from the baselines in three respects that could affect the comparison: it restarts attempts from frontier states, it replays and verifies composed paths, and it uses its own stopping rule. We address each in turn. 

Frontier restarts. Restarting from a frontier state requires a deterministic, resettable environment. We regard this as the setting T ROPIC is designed for, not an advantage granted to it. To check that restarts alone do not explain the gains, the ablations in Table 3 separate them from composition. T ROPIC (No Composition) keeps the archive and frontier restarts and trains by maximum likelihood on sampled verified paths, which makes it closely related to archive-based exploration combined with rejection-sampling fine-tuning. It reaches 40 .8% on Sokoban, on par with the strongest baseline (SNR-Aware, 

42 .2% ). Conversely, T ROPIC (Root-Only) removes frontier restarts but keeps composition, and reaches 51 .8% , above every baseline. The improvement therefore comes from composing fragments across attempts, not from restarts. 

Replay and verification. Replaying a composed path executes its stored actions in the environment and queries the verifier. Accordingly, it generates no tokens. Since all methods generate the same number of rollouts per iteration, replay adds only environment calls, which are inexpensive relative to generation in all four tasks. 

Stopping and Wall-Clock Time. All methods share the same iteration cap. The baselines stop according to RAGEN-2’s criteria, while T ROPIC stops early when no training problem yields a new update. Reported wall-clock times therefore reflect the end-to-end cost of each method under its standard protocol, not a comparison at matched compute. This does not favor T ROPIC . Since no method can exceed the shared cap, early stopping can only shorten T ROPIC ’s training, never extend it, and each baseline trains for as long as its standard protocol allows. Running every method under its standard protocol is also the setting in which a practitioner would compare them. Success rates are unaffected by this difference: all methods are evaluated at their final checkpoint on the same held-out instances. 

## D LIMITATIONS 

Tropical RL changes a single operation of reinforcement learning: alternative trajectories are com-bined by a maximum instead of a sum. This one change gives the value three properties that expected return lacks. It names a concrete path, it never decreases as new verified paths are found, and it splits into a best prefix and a best suffix at any shared state. T ROPIC uses these properties to assemble solutions from fragments the actor never produced together. On four agentic tasks, this translates into higher single-attempt success than on-policy baselines. It is worth being precise about the guarantee. The archive, and the capability it certifies, never shrink. For an actor with separate parameters per state, one step on the best path raises the tropical value of every state in the graph (Theorem 4). A language model, however, shares parameters across states and problems, so a step on one problem can still lower the likelihood of a verified path on another. The coverage paths of Section 4.3 mitigate this, but they are a heuristic, not a proof. T ROPIC therefore guarantees that no verified computation is lost from memory , not that the actor never forgets. This distinction points to the direction we find most promising: continual self-improvement, in which a capability, once verified, is kept and becomes reusable by later ones. Expected return has no mechanism for this, since it stores discoveries only in current probabilities and normalization makes them compete (Proposition 1). The tropical value provides the missing bookkeeping: a set of verified computations that only grows and whose best elements compose. Whether this record can be transferred into shared weights without interference, at scale, remains open. 30
