Abstract 

Relational databases are among the most widely deployed forms of structured knowl-edge, and natural language access to them re-quires grounding language onto schema enti-ties and relations while handling the ambiguity inherent in how people phrase requests. Exist-ing synthetic NL-to-SQL data generation meth-ods largely ignore this ambiguity and produce oversimplified queries that fail to prepare mod-els for the complexity of real-world structured knowledge access. We present FALCON, a framework that generates realistic, ambiguity-aware NL-to-SQL data matching the complex-ity of challenging real-world benchmarks, at low cost using compact open models. Our ap-proach combines reserved-word SQL seeding and persona-based prompting to generate struc-turally complex queries, while alignment-based filtering preserves difficulty by distinguishing genuinely incorrect examples from complex but valid queries. Human evaluation confirms con-sistent high quality across model sizes, and our generated data exceeds existing benchmarks in both SQL complexity and natural language richness. Difficulty-stratified analysis shows models trained on FALCON data increasingly outperform baseline-trained models as query complexity increases, validating our pipeline’s success in generating challenging training data. When combined with a small proportion of ex-isting benchmark data, mixed training recovers performance on simpler queries while preserv-ing these advantages on complex ones. The model- and database-agnostic design enables organizations to generate high-complexity NL-to-SQL training data locally without external APIs.

Existing synthetic data generation ap-proaches face a critical trade-off: they either rely on expensive frontier model APIs, making them cost-prohibitive for many organizations, or pro-duce oversimplified queries that fail to capture production-level complexity (Lupidi et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). 2.1 The Academic-Industry Gap in NL-to-SQL 

Early NL-to-SQL datasets established foundational benchmarks but with significant limitations for real-world deployment. WikiSQL (Zhong et al., 2017; Yavuz et al., 2018) focused on single-relation queries, while Spider (Yu et al., 2018) and BIRD (Li et al., 2023) introduced cross-domain complex-ity and professional-domain examples. However, recent work has identified critical gaps between benchmark performance and production viability, including ambiguous queries from informal lan-guage use and underspecification, and database complexity at enterprise scale (Floratou et al., 2024). 

2.2 The Cost Barrier to Industry Deployment 

Beyond benchmark-to-production gaps, cost effi-ciency and privacy present fundamental barriers to enterprise adoption. While frontier LLMs demon-strate strong performance, their API costs make continuous deployment prohibitive (Hoang et al., 2025). Fine-tuning smaller models offers signifi-cant cost and privacy advantages (Luo et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) and can outperform prompting (Li et al., 2024a), but requires substantial high-quality training data. Organizations need production-ready systems handling complex queries and diverse lan-guage (Li et al., 2024a), yet generating training data via expensive frontier models or human annotators undermines local deployment benefits. 

2.3 Synthetic Data Generation for NL-to-SQL 

Researchers have explored automated synthesis to overcome scalability limitations. Source2Synth (Lupidi et al., 2025) combines WikiSQL tables with LLM-generated facts using execution-based verification, but requires fine-tuning multi-billion parameter models for filtering and focuses only on single-table queries. OmniSQL (Li et al., 2025) expands to more difficult queries and diverse lin-guistic styles, yet lacks ambiguity handling and requires larger model ensembles. SQLForge (Guo et al., 2025) introduces SQL template parsing and iterative domain exploration but relies on expen-sive closed-source models (GPT-4) with declining execution rates on complex queries. 

2.4 Handling Query Ambiguity 

Addressing ambiguous queries is critical for pro-duction reliability (Floratou et al., 2024), yet most synthetic datasets generate only unambigu-ous pairs. Know What I Don’t Know (Wang et al., 2023a) categorizes unanswerable questions but fo-cuses strictly on schema-level ambiguity, excluding broader linguistic ambiguities. Ambrosia (Saparina and Lapata, 2024) identifies database-independent ambiguities (scope, attachment, vagueness) that reflect real user behavior but remain underrepre-sented in training data (Floratou et al., 2024). 

Implications While these existing methods ad-dress specific facets of NL-to-SQL generation, there remains a need for a unified, cost-efficient framework that handles complexity diversity and ambiguity; we address these gaps with the FAL-CON pipeline described in the following section. 

3 Pipeline 

Our pipeline follows a multi-stage generation and filtering process: (1) SQL generation with reserved word and persona seeding, followed by executabil-ity filtering; (2) NL generation with LLM-as-a-judge candidate selection; (3) ambiguity detection and multiple query generation for questions that are ambigious; (4) alignment-based filtering to remove misaligned NL-SQL pairs; and (5) final prompt-based filtering to remove any remaining low-quality instances (Figure 1). We evaluate generation com-ponents by their contribution to SQL complexity and NL richness, and filtering components by their ability to retain high-quality examples while re-moving low-quality ones (validated against human annotations). 

3.1 SQL Query and NL Question Generation 

We adopt SQL-then-NL generation (Hu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2021; Lupidi et al., 2025), which empiri-cally increases both SQL and NL complexity (Ta-ble 1). The LLM is prompted with a table schema to generate a SQL query, including foreign key re-lationships when present. Each query is executed against the database with up to three attempts to ensure validity before discarding. The LLM is then prompted with the vali-dated query and schema to generate four candi-date NL questions via chain-of-thought prompt-ing (Prompt 19). 3.1.1 Seeding Techniques 

To mitigate the inherent repetitiveness of synthetic data (Wang et al., 2023b), we interleave two seed-ing techniques to encourage the LLM to generate unique results (Table 1). 

Persona Seeding. Inspired by Jandaghi et al. (2024); Ge et al. (2025), for each table, we prompt the LLM to generate a realistic user persona with specific goals and example questions. We sam-ple between two prompts to ensure a mix of com-mon personas (e.g., "a software engineer") and cre-ative ones (e.g., "an indie tabletop racing game designer"). This improves the complexity and di-versity of generated questions while ensuring var-ied use cases (Table 1, Prompt 16, Prompt 17, Prompt 18, Prompt 20).

Reserved Word Seeding. 3.1.3 Alignment-Based Filtering 

Generative models frequently produce semantically plausible questions misaligned with SQL logic. We train an alignment prediction model on 9,000+ BIRD-derived examples (50% systematically cor-rupted) using a novel hybrid metric combining se-mantic similarity (40%) and schema entity match-ing (60%) with corruption penalties. This scoring metric achieves Pearson correlation r = 0 .70 to human judgments (Appendix A.6). ModernBERT trained on this metric achieves MSE: 0.0203 and MAE: 0.1129, outperforming other models (Ap-pendix Table 11) and filtering methods (Table 2). This filtering step verifies that the generated query is faithfully grounded in the schema entities and re-lations the question implies, rejecting semantically plausible but ungrounded pairs. 

3.2 Multi-Interpretation Generation and Filtering 

Because ambiguity resolution is central to reliably constructing structured knowledge from natural lan-guage, we explicitly model and generate alternative interpretations rather than filtering ambiguous ex-amples away. For queries flagged as ambiguous, we prompt the model to identify the ambiguity type (Saparina and Lapata, 2024) and generate an alternative SQL interpretation (Prompt 25). The alternative is validated with an ambiguity-specific prompt filter (Prompt 23, 24); the model has three attempts to produce an executable alternative that passes this filter, otherwise the example proceeds as unambiguous. All examples then undergo a final prompt-based filter verifying that: (1) the SQL validly inter-prets the NL, (2) the SQL is sensible and non-redundant, and (3) the NL resembles something a human would actually ask (Prompt 26). While the reward model achieves high precision (Table 2), it only reaches 71% specificity and was trained solely on alignment, missing aligned examples with poor NL or SQL quality. Prompt-based filtering addresses these limitations and adapts to ambiguity-specific criteria beyond the reward model’s scope. Examples passing all checks are added to the final dataset. 

4 Evaluation 

4.1 Difficulty Metrics 

We measure NL Richness as a weighted combi-nation of linguistic complexity features (Balyan et al., 2020; Byrd and Srivastava, 2022): mean to-kens before main verb (15%), constituents per word (30%), subordinate clauses (30%), and coordinate clauses (20%). SQL complexity is quantified using SQLGlot to parse query structures, with weighted features: words and columns (10% each), and joins, sub-queries, operators, and splits (20% each) (Yu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023; Vashistha and Jain, 2014). 4.2 Human Evaluation 

We conduct human evaluation with 4 annotators using the rubric in Appendix A.9, which separately grades NL quality, SQL quality, and alignment to distinguish failure modes (e.g., inefficient queries vs. misaligned NL-SQL pairs). Each annotator independently scored 100 disjoint examples, with disagreements resolved through structured group discussion following standard adjudication proto-col. 

4.3 Downstream Task Evaluation 

We finetune Qwen 2.5 3B and 1.5B (Appendix A.11, A.12) on three training sets: (1) 5,500 syn-thetic examples, (2) 5,500 Spider examples, and (3) mixed data (95% synthetic, 5% Spider). Models are evaluated on our synthetic test set ( n = 2 ,000 )and Spider test set ( n = 2 ,147 ) with consistent hyperparameters. We employ three automatic metrics: Exact Match (EM) , direct string equivalence (Yu et al., 2018); Fuzzy Score (FS) , average string similarity; and Soft Execution Accuracy (Soft-EX) , F1 score of result set overlap (Li et al., 2024b). Fuzzy met-rics capture semantically equivalent queries with minor syntactic variations. We conduct difficulty-stratified analysis across query complexity levels to characterize model performance on queries of varying difficulty. 

5 Ablations 

5.1 Generation Order and Component Ablations 

To maximize SQL complexity and NL richness, we ablated generation order (SQL-then-NL vs. NL-then-SQL), seeding methods (persona and reserved words), and LLM-as-a-judge selection strategies for NL: a Compare Judge (selecting the best candi-date NL, Prompt 21) and a Score Judge (iterative regeneration of NL until meeting a quality thresh-old, Prompt 22). This NL candidate selection oc-curs before the final alignment-based filtering (Sec-tion 3.1.3). Table 1 shows that SQL-then-NL outperforms the reverse order. Reserved word seeding improves SQL complexity while persona seeding improves NL richness. The Compare Judge yields the high-est NL richness and unexpectedly improves SQL complexity by pruning simpler queries associated with low-quality questions. We therefore adopt SQL-then-NL generation, interleave both seeding methods, and use the Compare Judge in our final pipeline.

Analysis. From this analysis we draw three con-clusions. First, approximately one third of flagged errors are acceptable variations that would not mis-lead a real user, meaning our reported Spider ac-curacy is meaningfully underestimated. This is a known limitation of both string match and execu-tion match metrics. This underestimation likely affects our model more than Spider-trained base-lines, since style-based acceptable variations are more probable when training and test distributions diverge substantially. Second, spurious predicate hallucination and result boundary errors are the most common genuine failure modes, which we attribute to the overrepresentation of complex SQL queries in our dataset. We anticipate that mixing the training distribution with simpler data has the most impact here by reducing these artifacts, which is why we recommend seeding with 5% existing data (ex. from Spider or WikiSQL) to ensure sim-pler queries are represented. Third, schema entity confusion is more likely a consequence of how the model processes condensed schema information under context pressure than of our dataset compo-sition: when training the model, we compressed schema information for all tables into the prompt, requiring the model to resolve column and table references under significant context pressure, inde-pendently of the underlying NL-to-SQL pairs.

FALCON requires no external API access, enabling organizations to generate training data locally with-out per-query costs or privacy exposure. Using on average 5 LLM calls per example across 5 pipeline stages, generating 5,500 examples requires approx-imately 34.47 million input tokens and 5.13 mil-lion output tokens. At GPT-5.5 pricing, an equiva-lent run would cost approximately $326, versus $20 for an open model on a commodity GPU (Vast.ai, $0.15/hr), an over 16 × reduction, before accounting for the privacy implications of trans-mitting proprietary schemas to third-party APIs (Appendix A.4). 

7 Conclusion 

We present FALCON, a model- and database-agnostic framework for generating high-complexity NL-to-SQL data at low cost using compact open models. Through reserved-word seeding, persona-based prompting, and alignment-based filtering, FALCON generates data exceeding existing bench-marks in SQL complexity and natural language richness while maintaining high quality (>0.92 hu-man evaluation across all models). Our ambiguity handling addresses a critical gap in synthetic gener-ation, producing diverse data reflecting real-world linguistic challenges. Models trained on FALCON-generated data out-perform Spider-trained models as query complexity increases. FALCON’s ambiguity-aware, schema-grounded approach to NL-to-SQL generation offers a template for producing training data for natural language interfaces to structured knowledge more broadly, and its local, model-agnostic design keeps this accessible to organizations without reliance on frontier APIs. 

8 Limitations 

FALCON is intentionally designed to target the complex end of the query distribution, where exist-ing datasets and template-based methods already provide adequate coverage of simpler queries. This focus leads to an underrepresentation of simpler queries, as shown in Figure 9, and models trained solely on FALCON data achieve only 0.8% EM on Spider, requiring 5–10% real data supplemen-tation for broad applicability. Ambiguity detec-tion achieves high specificity (98.9%) but low re-call (28.6%), missing many genuinely ambiguous queries. FALCON currently supports SQLite only; ex-tension to other dialects requires dialect-specific reserved word distributions and syntax constraints. Evaluation is limited to public benchmarks (Spi-der, WikiSQL, BIRD), and production databases may exhibit complexity dimensions not captured academically. Resource constraints limited down-stream evaluation to compact models, though these suffice for high-quality generation ( >0.92 human evaluation scores). Generated data inherits domain coverage from seed databases. 

Ethical Considerations 

Domain coverage reflects source databases (Spi-der and WikiSQL), potentially inheriting domain-specific biases. The database-agnostic design en-ables organizations to generate data for their spe-cific use cases. AI tools were used to aid in non-essential writing and code generation.

A.1 Detailed Pipeline Overview 

We demonstrate the pipeline step by step using a running example. Note that all examples are sim-plified and made up for illustration purposes only; they are not taken directly from the pipeline output. A detailed diagram of the full pipeline is available in Figure 6, and step-by-step walkthroughs show-ing intermediate outputs for both an ambiguous and a non-ambiguous example are shown in Figures 7 and 8 respectively. 

Table: The pipeline begins with an existing dataset of table schemas, such as WikiSQL, and it-erates through them sequentially. For this example, we use the table shown in Table 8, which contains information about Australian license plates. 

Seeding and SQL Query Generation: For each table, the pipeline selects one of two diversity in-jection methods with equal probability: reserved word seeding or persona seeding. 

Reserved word seeding controls SQL complex-ity by specifying a target number of SQL reserved words for the generated query. This count is sampled from a probability distribution derived from BIRD’s training set, so that the synthetic data mirrors the complexity distribution of human-authored queries. The count is passed directly into the generation prompt (Prompt 15), and chain-of-thought reasoning is used to help the model adhere to the constraint. For example, target-ing three reserved words might produce: SELECT "State/Territory" FROM "table-1" WHERE "Text/background Color" = ’black/white’ .

Persona seeding instead grounds generation in a specific user’s goals and domain context. An LLM is prompted to generate a persona for the given table, for example, “License plate designer observ-ing trends by region,” along with associated goals (e.g., “To create the most sought-after license plate in Australia”) and example queries (e.g., “Back-ground color of Northern Territory vs. New South Wales”). The persona is randomly selected to be ei-ther a common professional type or a creative niche type to encourage diversity in register and termi-nology (Prompts 17 and 18). All three components — persona, goals, and example queries — are in-cluded in the SQL generation prompt (Prompt 16). It is important to note that reserved word seed-ing only influences SQL generation, while per-sona seeding influences both SQL and NL gen-eration, ensuring consistency between the query State/Territory Text/bg Color Format Current Slogan Current Series Notes

For the remainder of this example, we as-sume reserved word seeding produced: SELECT "State/Territory" FROM "table-1" WHERE "Text/background Color" = ’black/white’ ,(ie, selecting states for which the background color of the license plate is black and white). 

NL Question Generation: The validated SQL query and table schema are used to generate a nat-ural language question. When persona seeding is used, the persona and goals are also included, and the model is instructed to phrase the question in the voice of that persona, adjusting formality, domain-specific vocabulary, and whether the user would refer to columns by name or by colloquial proxy (Prompt 20). Without persona seeding, a standard chain-of-thought prompt is used (Prompt 19). In both cases, four candidate questions are generated simultaneously, and a compare judge selects the best one based on naturalness, accuracy, and com-pleteness (Prompt 21). For our example, the se-lected question might be: “Which states use black and white as their license plate colors?” 

Ambiguity Detection and Handling: The NL question is scored by a DistilBERT-based ambigu-ity model trained on Ambrosia data (Saparina and Lapata, 2024), producing a continuous score from 0 to 10. This score determines how the example proceeds through the next stage. 

If the score is 7 or above , the question is considered potentially ambiguous and enters the multi-interpretation branch. A prompt-based fil-ter (Prompts 23 and 24) first confirms whether the ambiguity is truly present. Specifically, it tests whether it falls into one of four categories: Scope, Attachment, Entity Vagueness, or Broadness. The first three categories and their accompanying exam-ples in the prompt are adapted from Saparina and Lapata (2024). Broadness is an additional category we introduce, inspired by Floratou et al. (2024), to capture cases where the NL is simply underspeci-fied (e.g., “best player,” where it is unclear whether this means most points scored or highest rating). If the filter determines the question is not genuinely ambiguous, the example is discarded. If ambiguity is confirmed, the pipeline attempts to generate a second SQL query representing a distinct valid in-terpretation of the same question (Prompt 25). The model must also identify the ambiguity type and ex-plain how the new query differs semantically from the original. The new query is tested for executabil-ity, and then passed through the same prompt filter again to verify that it is a genuinely different valid reinterpretation rather than a superficial variant. The pipeline makes up to three attempts to produce an acceptable alternative; if none succeed, the ex-ample proceeds as a standard unambiguous pair using only the original query. For our example, “Which states use black and white as their license plate colors?” is ambiguous: “black and white” could mean plates that are both black and white together, or plates that are either black or white. The two valid SQL interpretations would be an AND condition and an OR condition respectively, both of which are executable and se-mantically distinct. 

If the score is below 7 , the example skips the multi-interpretation branch and proceeds directly to the alignment model, which assigns a score be-tween 0 and 1 reflecting how well the NL question and SQL query correspond to each other. Exam-ples scoring 0.6 or above are retained. Note that ambiguous examples do not go through the align-ment reward model, as this model was not trained to handle ambiguity. This is why they are filtered separately using prompts. 

Final Quality Filter: Regardless of which path an example took through the ambiguity stage, all examples pass through a final prompt-based filter (Prompt 26) before being added to the dataset. This filter checks three things independently: that the SQL is a valid interpretation of the NL, that the SQL is non-redundant and well-formed, and that the NL sounds like something a real user would ask. This step catches any remaining low-quality examples that passed the alignment model but still contain subtle issues, such as alligned pairs where the question did not make sense to begin with (ex. taking the sum of a categorical column), the SQL is overly convoluted, or any other issues. 

A.2 Error Analysis 

We conducted a failure mode analysis on 100 randomly sampled incorrect predictions from the Qwen 2.5-3B model finetuned on our pipeline’s generations, evaluated on the Spider test set. The following taxonomy and occurrence counts were derived from this sample. 

Taxonomy. We identify nine error categories: 

Acceptable variation: The predicted SQL is semantically equivalent to the gold query or repre-sents a valid alternative interpretation of the natural language question. This includes cases where an ORDER BY clause is added but not requested, an additional column is returned that is directly im-plied by the NL question (e.g., the question asks which player scored the most goals and the query returns both player name and goal count), a redun-dant but non-harmful condition is present, or minor syntactic differences exist that do not affect the result set. These cases would fail string match or execution match but have no meaningful impact on the user’s informational need. 

Spurious predicate hallucination: The model introduces a WHERE clause condition that has no basis in the natural language question. Example: the question asks for all employees in a department, but the query adds WHERE status = ‘active’ 

with no such qualifier in the NL. 

Result boundary error: The model produces the correct logic but returns too much or too little. For example, limiting to 5 results when the user implies all results, or applying incorrect offsets. 

Schema entity confusion: The model maps a natural language entity to the wrong table or col-umn. Example: the question asks for “salary” but the query selects “bonus” because both are numeric compensation columns. 

Logical negation failure: The model fails to capture a negation expressed in the NL (e.g., “not”, “except”, “other than”), producing a query that re-trieves the complement of the intended set. Exam-ple: “Who did NOT win the award?” returns all winners instead of all non-winners. 

Degenerate join structure: The model pro-duces a join that is structurally malformed, redun-dant, or semantically incorrect. For example, join-ing a table to itself unnecessarily or using the wrong join key. 

Cross-row constraint flattening: Questions like “both X and Y” require set intersection across rows, but the model instead treats it as a condi-tion on a single row. Example: “Flights that took off from Florida and New York” becomes WHERE takeoff=‘Florida’ AND takeoff=‘New York’ ,which can never be true on a single row. 

Hardcoded quantifier substitution: The model sees a superlative such as “highest” or “fewest” and instead of computing the answer from the data, substitutes a hardcoded number. Example: “rated higher than average” becomes WHERE rating > 4 ,which will be wrong on any dataset where the true average differs. 

NL misinterpretation: The model misreads the core intent of the NL question and produces a query that answers a related but distinct question. Exam-ple: “How many orders were placed last month?” is answered with a list of all orders rather than a count.

clauses, etc.) The resulting dataset contains over 9,000 examples (median alignment: 0.72, IQR: 0.54-0.90). 

Alignment Score Computation. For each ex-ample, we compute a hybrid alignment score 

y ∈ [0 , 1] by comparing the generated question to BIRD’s human-written reference question. Be-cause BIRD questions align with their SQL queries, similarity to the reference indicates how well the generated question aligns with the SQL. Traditional semantic similarity (e.g., Sentence-BERT) often misses critical differences; For instance, "return the average" and "return the highest" may score simi-larly despite requiring different aggregations (AVG vs. MAX). Our metric combines two weighted components: 1. Semantic Similarity (40%): Sentence-BERT embeddings measuring linguistic proximity. 2. Schema Entity Scoring (60%): Extracts enti-ties (table/column names, literal values) from the SQL query and detects their mentions in the NL question. Applies multiplicative penal-ties ( 0.7n) for missing ground-truth entities or incorrect entities. For corrupted examples, we apply an additional 10% penalty per corruption (Figure 10).
