Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on offline agent trajectories is the standard approach for training specialized tool-using agents, but forcing models to imitate reasoning and actions token by token may harm other capabilities (e.g., general reasoning, tool calling, code generation) of the base model. In this work, we focus on studying how to better balance the trade-off between acquiring new capabilities and preserving existing ones during agent trace SFT . By comparing several baselines in our setup, standard SFT improves the target benchmark while lowering several non-target benchmark scores; meanwhile, simply constraining distributional drift using KL penalty or limiting the update magnitude did not avoid this regression trend. Motivated by recent token-wise adaptive learning objectives, this work proposes Privilege-Guided SFT (PG-SFT) to leverage turn-level information gain of agent trajectories as an indicator to adjust supervision strength. PG-SFT yields a more favorable observed trade-off on the evaluated benchmarks, substantially reducing distributional drift and broad capability degradation at the cost of slight degradation in target-task performance. Our findings suggest that balancing the acquisition–retention trade-off depends not only on whether the model is anchored to its base behavior, but also on where and how strongly supervision should depart from that behavior.

1 Introduction 

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has been the standard approach for adapting large language models (LLMs) to follow instructions and perform specialized tasks [Ouyang et al., 2022, Touvron et al., 2023]. For tool-using agents, offline execution trajectories provide supervision on reasoning and actions, allowing models to acquire task-specific behaviors without repeatedly interacting with expensive environments during training [Yao et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2024, Zeng et al., 2023]. However, token-level imitation treats every demonstrated assistant token as a target, regardless of whether it represents a task-relevant decision or an incidental realization of the trajectory. Fine-tuning can thus improve the target workflow while changing capabilities acquired before adaptation, a tension related to catastrophic forgetting [Kirkpatrick et al., 2017, Li and Hoiem, 2016, Kemker et al., 2018]. Regressions in knowledge, reasoning, instruction following, and safety-related behavior have also been observed in LLM post-training [Luo et al., 2023, Qi et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2026, Xu et al., 2026, Lin et al., 2025, Huan et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2024, Biderman et al., 2024]. Prior approaches reduce model–data mismatch through self-distillation [Yang et al., 2024] or constrain changes from the base model through reference-based objectives [Zhu et al., 2026a, Xu et al., 2026], but whether such control suffices to preserve capabilities remains undemonstrated; moreover, they focus on reasoning and instruction-following tasks, leaving capability 1arXiv:2610.00949v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 retention in agent fine-tuning largely unexplored. In this work, we study the following question: How does supervision-objective design affect the empirical trade-off between target adaptation and non-target benchmark performance? 

Intuitively, SFT may disrupt existing capabilities less if the student stays close to its base distribution—by anchoring predictions to it or limiting successive update sizes. Yet, a model that changes too little may retain its old behavior without acquiring the target behavior. We examine this tension by holding the offline trajectories fixed and varying only the supervision objective: standard SFT provides the full-imitation reference; SFT with a Base-model KL penalty tests direct anchoring to the base distribution; and proximal SFT (PSFT) tests a constraint on updates relative to a lagged policy [Zhu et al., 2026b]. Together, these methods ask whether conservative learning suffices for balancing capability acquisition and retention. Unfortunately, extensive experiments on Qwen3 [Qwen Team, 2025] and Qwen3.5 [Qwen Team, 2026] across four benchmarks show that better fitting of the training data can coincide with larger distributional deviation from the base model, yet simply reducing this deviation with a fixed KL constraint does not necessarily produce better downstream capability retention. Trust-region baseline PSFT keeps the model closest to the base distribution, but acquires the least of the target behavior. Recently, token-wise supervision adjustment DFT [Wu et al., 2026] and InfoSFT [Sabbaghi et al., 2026] yielded good performance in reasoning and math benchmarks. Although our preliminary analyses reveal DFT’s incompatibility with agent SFT, their insights on adaptive supervision motivate us to introduce 

Privilege-Guided Supervised Fine-Tuning (PG-SFT) . PG-SFT allocates supervision turn by turn, matching the granularity at which agent decisions appear, and uses “next actions” in trajectories as training-time privileged information to measure the “knowledge gain” imposed by the hint, adjusting the supervision intensity of that turn accordingly. On Qwen3.5-4B, PG-SFT matches SFT on the selected target SWE-bench subset (59/89, pass@3) while retaining stronger performance on other non-target benchmarks. On Qwen3-4B-Thinking, PG-SFT gives up limited target-task performance in exchange for stronger retention on non-target tasks. These results suggest that the key of balancing the trade-off is not only regularizing supervision toward the base model, but deciding where and how strongly the model should depart from it. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• An empirical study of capability acquisition and retention in offline agent fine-tuning. We formulate post-training as a joint learning–retention problem and evaluate adaptation not only through target-task performance, but also through target fitting, distributional drift from the base model, and retained non-target capabilities. 

• PG-SFT as an adaptive probe of the learning–retention trade-off. We introduce Privilege-Guided SFT, which uses privileged conditioning to estimate the intensity of moving away from the base distribution. PG-SFT provides a concrete mechanism for testing whether adaptive, example-dependent learning intensity can achieve a more favorable balance between acquiring specialized behavior and retaining existing capabilities. 

• A controlled comparison of different learning pressures. Using the same offline trajectories, we compare ordinary SFT, SFT with a fixed Base-KL constraint, a clipped-ratio trust-region objective PSFT, and an adaptive supervision strategy PG-SFT. The comparison shows that fitting the target distribution, remaining close to the base distribution, and preserving downstream capabilities are distinct properties; optimizing one does not automatically guarantee the others. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 presents the compared objectives and PG-SFT; Section 4 reports the acquisition–retention analyses; Section 5 analyzes the turn-level coefficient and discusses the scope of our study. 22 Related Work 

2.1 Capability retention in post-training 

Fine-tuning enables language models to acquire specialized behaviors, but adaptation can also modify capabilities that are not directly optimized Lin et al. [2025], Huan et al. [2025], Wang et al. [2024]: continual fine-tuning studies document forgetting of previously acquired knowledge and tasks Luo et al. [2023], Biderman et al. [2024], and alignment-oriented investigations show that task-specific adaptation may affect safety-related behaviors Qi et al. [2024]. Existing mitigations either constrain deviation from the initial model through reference-based objectives or update rules Yang et al. [2024], Zhu et al. [2026a], Xu et al. [2026], Zhu et al. [2026b], Li et al. [2018], or preserve the original parameters through additional components Wang et al. [2026], Shenfeld et al. [2025]. 

2.2 Model-aware supervision and adaptive learning pressure 

Several observations suggest that supervision should be calibrated to the learner: data fitting the learner is more effective Zhang et al. [2025], self-distillation reduces model–data mismatch by generating supervision closer to the learner distribution Yang et al. [2024], and smaller models struggle to absorb reasoning behaviors from substantially stronger teachers Li et al. [2025]. Some work curates the data by it self to improve data-model alignment, including, quality filtering Chen et al. [2023], Liu et al. [2024b], difficulty grading Li et al. [2024a], Liu et al. [2024a], and data rewriting Li et al. [2024b], though such gains do not always scale to large pools Xia et al. [2025]. Some recent studies proposed to adjust supervision strength non-uniformly to better match the data-model relationship: DFT scales token-level updates by the model’s confidence on demonstrations Wu et al. [2026], IDFT accounts for contextual distribution differences Zhang et al. [2026], anchored SFT combines adaptive weighting with reference-model regularization Zhu et al. [2026a], CADFT estimates example-level compatibility Zhou et al. [2026], and TALR reweights per-token losses to reduce interference with general capabilities Lin et al. [2025]. PSFT also adapts the contribution of supervised tokens, but its signal derives from the magnitude of previous model updates Zhu et al. [2026b]. 

2.3 Privileged information as a training-time signal 

Learning using privileged information studies settings where additional information is available during training but unavailable during inference Vapnik and Izmailov [2015], and generalized distillation shows that such training-only signals improve prediction when they inform the target mapping Lopez-Paz et al. [2016]. Privileged information is not universally beneficial, however: stronger privileged features do not necessarily yield better student models, motivating selective use of privileged signals Yang et al. [2022], Aslam et al. [2023]; recent analyses further find that privileged teachers can bias solutions Harne et al. [2026], suppress self-correction Kaur et al. [2026], or yield unstable gains Shrestha and Tessier [2026], and that more privileged information is not automatically better Zhao et al. [2026b]. In language models, privileged signals have been explored through context distillation and self-distillation mechanisms Snell et al. [2022], Agarwal et al. [2024], Zhao et al. [2026a], Penaloza et al. [2026]. 

3 Trade-off Balancing: A Supervision Objective Perspective 

This section investigates the acquisition–retention tension through a controlled comparison of training objectives. All objectives are trained on identical data with identical supervised tokens, so that only the objective varies. We develop the comparison along two lines. The conservative line restrains the overall fitting strength with a single uniform rule, asking whether more conservative learning alone can balance acquisition against retention. The adaptive line instead varies supervision strength across decisions; our first attempt in this direction, DFT [Wu et al., 2026], underperforms even the untrained base model. Inspired by 3some preliminary results, we propose PG-SFT, a turn-level adaptive objective that allocates supervision with training-time privileged information.

In an agent trajectory, a turn couples reasoning with the action it produces, making an agent turn the smallest unit in which a complete decision appears. 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate the acquisition–retention trade-off among the supervision objectives; evaluation metrics, benchmark construction, and training hyperparameters are in Appendices C, D, and B. 

Models and training data. We select two 4B-scale model families, Qwen3.5-4B Qwen Team [2026] and Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 Qwen Team [2025]. All fine-tuning objectives use the same offline agent trajectories sampled from the open-source Nemotron-SFT-SWE-v3 dataset NVIDIA [2026]; for Qwen3.5-4B, the main experiments use 1K randomly sampled OpenHands Wang et al. [2025] trajectories; for Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, we increase the training set to 5K to observe differentiable behavior after SFT. 

Benchmarks. SWE-bench Lite Jimenez et al. [2024] share similar trajectory format as the training data and serves as the primary target-task benchmark. GPQA Diamond Rein et al. [2024], BFCL v4 Patil et al. [2025], and LiveCodeBench Jain et al. [2025] evaluate capabilities retained during fine-tuning, from the aspects of general reasoning, multi-turn tool use, and code generation. Given the limited capability of 4B-scale backbones, some benchmarks are evaluated on their easier subsets to keep the scores informative. We report the pass@3 metric Chen et al. [2021] to alleviate the impact of random sampling of agentic tasks, which also aligns with the mode coverage effect of SFT Gu et al. [2024]. 

Baselines. We evaluate five models for each backbone: Base , SFT , SFT+Base-KL , PSFT , and PG-SFT ,our adaptive objective described in Section 3.4. The four fine-tuning objectives observe exactly the same training data and the same supervised assistant tokens. PSFT uses its published trust-region radius and optimization budget (Appendix B).

Results reveal a clear tension between fitting the target workflow and retaining capabilities already present in the Base model. Standard SFT improves the target task but regresses on all three non-target benchmarks. Adding a Base-KL regularizer reduces distributional deviation during training, but does not alleviate these downstream regressions. Thus, globally encouraging the model to remain close to its initial distribution is not sufficient to preserve capabilities. PSFT achieves one of the worst target-task results among the SFT objectives while retaining the most non-target capability among the baselines, aligning with findings that a trust-region constraint improves retention [Zhu et al., 2026b]; it nevertheless fails to learn effectively from the demonstrations. PG-SFT exhibits a different acquisition–retention profile. It resolves 59 of the 89 instances (66 .3%) on Qwen3.5, the highest target-task result we measure, while staying within a few instances of Base on non-target benchmarks. On Qwen3, PG-SFT gives up some target-task performance in exchange for stronger retention on non-target tasks. This balanced profile also holds in aggregate: PG-SFT attains the highest Macro Avg on both backbones (62 .10% and 55 .58%) and is the only fine-tuning objective that improves over its own Base model ( +1.49 pp and +2.07 pp), whereas every other objective loses capability overall ( −0.67 to −5.89 pp). Despite being trained on the same offline demonstrations, objectives can induce substantially different changes outside the target task: stronger imitation does not necessarily translate into a better overall adaptation outcome, and a fixed Base-KL constraint does not reliably determine which existing behaviors should be preserved. To understand factors that may contribute to these differences, we next examine how each objective influences the learning process and distribution updates. 

4.3 How Much Do Objectives Learn from the Data? 

We evaluate the distribution alignment of each fine-tuned model with the same 120 held-out agent trajectories from the Nemotron-SFT-SWE-v3 dataset. 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

Correlations between turn coefficient and turn length. 𝛼 𝑘 is an average value, and thus could strongly correlate with the number of tokens in a turn. We analyzed 5,503 assistant turns from 120 held-out trajectories to understand their relationship. While the turn length correlates with 𝐺 𝑘 and 𝐻 𝑘 individually ( 𝑟 = 0.66 and 0.75), the two dependencies largely cancel in the ratio, leaving only a weak correlation with 𝛼 𝑘 (Pearson 

𝑟 = 0.225, cluster-bootstrap 95% CI [0.184 , 0.269 ], univariate 𝑅 2 ≈ 5%). Therefore, despite the turn-level averaging of 𝛼 𝑘 , the coefficient reflects more on what a turn contains rather than how many tokens it has. 

Scope & Limitations. Our distributional measurements are empirical, and cannot establish a causal link between drift and retention. Our evaluation covers two related 4B-scale backbones, a single agent domain, and a limited number of training runs. Therefore, we cannot promise our findings can be extended to training data and model size at any scale: although fine-tuning regressions have been documented in larger models [Qi et al., 2024, Luo et al., 2023], data-selection effects are capacity-dependent [Dang et al., 2026], so neither a pure capacity account nor a pure objective account can be excluded. Finally, privileged scoring and full-vocabulary KL add computation over ordinary SFT, although the privileged scores are computed once for a dataset and a model. 

Conclusion. This work studies how supervision-objective design affects the acquisition–retention trade-off in offline agent fine-tuning. Comparing standard SFT, Base-KL, PSFT, and PG-SFT on identical trajectories shows that target fitting, base proximity, and downstream retention are distinct: SFT forgets non-target capabilities; Base-KL reduces drift without reliable retention gains; PSFT stays near the base model but learns little. PG-SFT improves the trade-off through adaptive, turn-level supervision. Balancing acquisition and retention thus depends not merely on anchoring to the base model, but on deciding where and how strongly to 10 depart from it.

C Evaluation protocol 

Benchmark sets. GPQA Diamond Rein et al. [2024] uses all 198 questions in the frozen set. BFCL v4 Patil et al. [2025] uses a fixed 200-instance subset of the multi-turn categories (50 per category) scored by the official bfcl-eval harness; this subset is a development split of the benchmark rather than its official test split. LiveCodeBench Jain et al. [2025] uses a frozen 120-instance stratified subset of release v6. SWE-bench Jimenez et al. [2024] uses the 89-instance set of Appendix D with the official SWE-bench harness verification. 

Metric. The target-task column reports pass@3 : an instance is counted as resolved when the harness resolves at least one of its attempts. The attempt schedule of each subset of the 89 instances is given in Appendix D. 

Generation budget. SWE-bench rollouts use temperature 1, at most 8192 new tokens per round, at most 150 rounds, and a context of 131072 tokens. GPQA is generated at temperature 0.6 with top-𝑝 0.95, top-𝑘 

20, and at most 32768 new tokens in the first pass; instances whose first output is truncated at that budget are re-run with a 65536-token budget. BFCL is generated at temperature 0 with seed 42, at most 8192 new tokens per request, and a context of 262144 tokens. 

Scoring. GPQA is graded by extracting the final answer under a fixed answer rule: an example counts as correct when the correct option is identifiable from the response, and examples without an identifiable final answer count as incorrect. SWE-bench, BFCL, and LiveCodeBench use their task-specific execution-based evaluators. 16 D SWE-bench evaluation set 

The target-task results in Table 1 are evaluated on a frozen set of 89 SWE-bench Lite instances. We construct this set to improve discriminability: on a uniformly random sample of Lite, the two backbones often achieve similar performance, providing limited signal for comparing training objectives. The final set combines a random subset with a difficulty-screened subset where the two backbones exhibit clearer separation. 

Official Lite split. SWE-bench Lite is constructed from the SWE-bench test set through a filtering and sampling procedure that removes low-quality or ambiguous instances and selects 300 test instances. 2 We follow the official harness and evaluation protocol; only the evaluated instance subset differs. 

Random subset. The random subset is the frozen 50-instance sample used in our earlier experiments, drawn from the 300 Lite instances with random.sample (seed 42). It is not difficulty-screened or repository-stratified. 

Difficulty-screened subset. The screened subset is constructed using external evaluation evidence without using any model evaluated in this paper. We aggregate public SWE-bench Lite evaluation records and repeated-sampling statistics to estimate instance-level solvability, remove known flaky instances, and select 50 instances with higher and more consistent resolution frequencies. The selected instances have average resolution frequencies of 62.1% and 64.0% under the two statistics, respectively. These values characterize the selected instances and should not be interpreted as model pass rates. 

Union and scoring. The two subsets overlap on 11 instances, resulting in an 89-instance evaluation set after deduplication by instance_id . All instances use the same agent scaffold, prompt template, and output budget. For the random subset, we follow the previous evaluation protocol; for the screened subset, we additionally evaluate up to three attempts for unresolved instances. Since additional attempts are allocated conditionally based on previous outcomes, the reported pass@3 should be viewed as a comparison protocol rather than an unbiased estimate of a pre-registered three-sample evaluation. The resulting 89-instance scores are not directly comparable to SWE-bench Lite leaderboard numbers, which are reported on the full 300-instance test set. 

E Future directions 

Four directions follow directly from the limitations discussed in Section 5. First, the design space of the hint itself is unexplored: its granularity, its source (the next action, environment feedback, or the final outcome), and counterfactual hints as a specificity check on the signal. Second, coefficients could be re-estimated against the current policy rather than the frozen base, tracking the diminishing returns of each turn as training proceeds. Third, turn-level allocation is orthogonal to rehearsal and to online reinforcement learning—where rollout outcomes provide privileged information essentially for free—and combining them is a natural next step. Fourth, comparing privileged gain against other turn-level signals (e.g., margin- or entropy-based scores) would isolate whether action attribution is necessary for the retention benefit.
