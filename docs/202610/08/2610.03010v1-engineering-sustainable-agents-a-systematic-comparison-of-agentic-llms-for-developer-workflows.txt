HUI SONG, SINTEF, Norway Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in software engineering, including agentic systems that coordinate multiple agents, but impose higher computational and environmental costs. In this paper, we present a comprehensive empirical study of agentic LLM systems across five software engineering tasks: code generation, technical debt identification, code vulnerability detection, log parsing, and log analysis. For each task, we compare LLM configurations that range from a non-agentic single-query baseline to multi-agent workflows, using six open-weight LLMs, two prompt strategies, and three hardware platforms. We assess each configuration in terms of accuracy, inference latency, and energy consumption. Our results reveal substantial trade-offs between agentic complexity and energy efficiency: multi-agent designs consume on average 6.36 ×

as much energy and run 6.07 × as long as the non-agentic baseline, with worst-case slowdowns of up to 160 × for individual task–hardware pairs. Accuracy gains from additional agents are limited and task-specific: multi-agent improves average vulnerability-detection accuracy, but lightweight non-agentic and single-agent configurations still dominate the Pareto front, accounting for 59 of 66 Pareto-optimal configurations. Model and prompt choice act as task-specific levers whose effective direction varies between tasks rather than as global defaults. We translate these findings into design guidelines for sustainable, task-aware LLM-based development tools.

These capabilities have been accessed through single-query interactions with general-purpose models. On the other hand, the rise of LLM-based multi-agent systems, where multiple specialized agents collaborate to tackle different parts of a task, promises to enhance reasoning, problem decomposition, and robustness in developer workflows [18]. Early work in this area, such as agent frameworks for secure code generation and multi-agent debate mechanisms, suggests potential benefits including improved code correctness, enhanced security, and better scalability [9, 27, 41, 47, 65]. At the same time, LLMs incur high environmental costs, including substantial energy consumption, water usage, and CO 2 emissions, with impacts sometimes magnitudes greater for reasoning-intensive prompts compared to simpler ones [26, 28, 48]. This highlights an urgent need to consider sustainability alongside performance when designing agentic LLM systems. 

Problem Statement. Despite the growing enthusiasm for LLM-based multi-agent systems in software engineering, the trade-offs between their effectiveness and sustainability remain under-investigated. Early work has demonstrated that agentic architectures, where autonomous agents orchestrate, refine, and reason collectively, can enhance task performance in domains like code generation and complex problem solving, with success rates increasing by up to 70% compared to single-agent approaches [51]. Similarly, literature reviews emphasize the transformative potential of such systems for autonomous software development workflows [18]. However, this enhanced capability often demands greater computational overhead. Previous studies have highlighted that 

LLM inference consumes substantial energy and contributes notably to CO 2 emissions ,especially for reasoning-intensive prompts [2, 10, 26, 48]. Crucially, no existing study simultaneously evaluates agentic LLM configurations across multiple tasks, models, and hardware platforms with respect to both performance (accuracy, latency) and environmental cost (energy consumption). Without such a multi-dimensional, empirically grounded evaluation, practitioners lack the evidence needed to design effective yet sustainable multi-agent development tools. This gap calls for a structured analysis that bridges performance gains with ecological and practical deployment considerations. 

Goal of This Study. The primary goal of this study is to systematically investigate the design, performance, and sustainability trade-offs of multi-agent LLM systems for automating software engineering tasks. Although multi-agent LLM architectures are increasingly studied across diverse domains, systematic empirical evidence on their effectiveness and efficiency in practical developer tasks remains scarce. This study aims to fill this gap by providing empirical evidence and actionable insights across a representative spectrum of software engineering tasks, including code generation , where LLMs are used to synthesize executable source code from natural language prompts; technical debt identification , which involves analyzing code snippets for signs of code smells; code vulnerability detection, which targets identifying security flaws or unsafe coding patterns; log parsing, where structured representations are extracted from raw log files; and log analysis, which involves interpreting parsed logs to detect errors or anomalous system behaviors. We select these five software engineering tasks because they represent a diverse set of challenges across the software development lifecycle. They vary in terms of input/output structure, reasoning complexity, and reliance on external tools, making them well-suited for evaluating the performance and scalability of different LLM configurations. This diversity enables a comprehensive assessment of how agentic system design impacts effectiveness, efficiency, and sustainability in real-world development scenarios. For each task, we implement a series of increasingly complex LLM-based configurations, i.e., non-agentic single-query models, single-agent setups, dual-agent configurations, and multi-agent workflows involving collective intelligence and division of labor among agents. These configurations are deployed using multiple families of LLMs (i.e., Gemma and its code-specialized variant CodeGemma, Qwen and its code-specialized variant Qwen-Coder,

Our goal is to evaluate each system setup along three critical dimensions: accuracy , to measure task-specific effectiveness; inference latency , to understand the responsiveness and feasibility of real-time deployment; and energy consumption , to assess the computational cost of running different configurations. We conduct this study with the expectation that trade-offs will emerge across multiple variation points, including the software engineering task, the underlying LLM model, the agentic system design, and the prompting strategy. Research Questions. We structure our study around four research questions (RQs), focusing on the effectiveness, efficiency, and sustainability of LLM-based agentic systems. Specifically, we investigate: 

• How do different LLM system configurations perform across software engineering tasks in terms of accuracy? (RQ1) 

• How do LLM system configurations differ in energy consumption across software engineering tasks and hardware platforms? (RQ2) 

• What is the inference latency of different agent configurations across software engineering tasks and hardware platforms? (RQ3) 

• What trade-offs exist between performance, energy efficiency, and latency across software engineering tasks and hardware platforms, and are there task-specific patterns that inform the design of sustainable multi-agent LLM systems? (RQ4) 

Our Findings. Our results consistently show that agentic complexity has bounded benefits and systematic costs. The non-agentic baseline (NA) has the highest average accuracy for four of the five tasks, while the multi-agent configuration (MA) performs best for vulnerability detection. Moving from NA to MA increases energy consumption by 6.36 × and latency by 6.07 × on average, with worst-case slowdowns of up to 160 ×. Of 66 Pareto-optimal configurations across all tasks, only one is multi-agent, and 36 (55%) are non-agentic. Agentic configuration is therefore a first-order factor, but accuracy gains from additional agents are task-specific while the cost increase is much more systematic. Model and prompt choice, in contrast, have task-specific effects whose direction depends on the task: no single model wins across tasks, and prompt strategy alone can swing log-parsing accuracy from 3.4% to 54.3%. Hardware effects are modest for lightweight workloads but become first-order for heavier configurations, where a high-end server can be 3.4 × faster than a workstation on MA. Energy and latency are tightly coupled (Pearson 𝑟 = 0.987 in log–log space), so optimizing one effectively optimizes the other. Together, these findings indicate that competitive LLM-based developer tools can be built with lightweight, task-aware configurations, and that multi-agent designs should be adopted only with concrete evidence of a task-specific benefit.

2 Background and Related Work 2.1 Multi-Agent LLM Systems 

Multi-agent systems (MAS) powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) are an emerging paradigm for enabling collaboration, specialization, and iterative reasoning across autonomous agents. In contrast to single-turn, single-agent interactions, multi-agent LLM systems leverage structured communication and role decomposition to handle complex tasks more effectively. Recent surveys and systematizations outline the architectural components of LLM-based multi-agent systems, including agent roles, memory, planning, and interaction protocols. Li et al. [36] provide a comprehensive taxonomy of multi-agent LLM capabilities, highlighting key challenges in scalability, coordination, and self-evolution. Similarly, Tran et al. [57] and Liu et al. [37] survey coordination strategies in multi-agent LLMs, offering categorizations of mechanisms and patterns that frame the design space of such systems. Several open-source agent frameworks have been developed to support these patterns. They provide infrastructure for task orchestration, agent communication, and integration with external tools or data sources. Examples include AutoGen [61], which enables multi-agent collaboration for solving complex tasks, and Semantic Kernel [50], which combines LLMs with modular plugins for scalable AI systems. AutoGPT [6] supports autonomous multi-step task execution through

planning and refinement. Similarly, OpenAgents [63] and AgentGPT [1] focus on deploying au-tonomous AI agents, emphasizing modularity, integration, and usability for real-world applications. These frameworks excel in enabling multi-agent collaboration, where specialized agents—such as those for code generation, result interpretation, or output refinement—work together to achieve shared objectives. Frameworks like AutoGen and AutoGPT decompose complex workflows into manageable tasks, enhancing the efficiency and flexibility of LLM-powered systems. Interoperabil-ity is another critical feature, allowing seamless integration with external databases, APIs, and knowledge graphs. Tools like OpenAgents , AgentGPT , and Semantic Kernel streamline the processing of heterogeneous data, aligning real-time and historical information with system-specific knowledge to generate actionable insights. Multi-agent LLM systems represent a significant evolution in AI-assisted software engineering, offering new capabilities for structured collaboration and task specialization. While frameworks such as AutoGen, OpenAgents, and AgentGPT provide a foundation for their development, rigorous empirical evaluation, particularly with respect to performance-efficiency trade-offs and environ-mental impact, is still lacking. This paper contributes to filling that gap by systematically assessing multi-agent configurations across software engineering tasks, models, and hardware platforms. 

2.2 Multi-Agent LLM Systems in Software Engineering 

Recent advancements in LLM-based MAS have sparked considerable interest in the software engineering community, owing to their enhanced problem-solving capabilities and structured collaboration patterns [18]. For instance, multi-agent frameworks built on LLMs have significantly advanced the field of automated code generation [18, 29]. These approaches decompose the complex process of software synthesis into specialized sub-tasks, such as planning, coding, testing, and de-bugging, handled by collaborating agents, thereby addressing limitations in coherence, correctness, and scalability inherent to single-agent LLM systems. AgentCoder by Huang et al. [22] exemplifies this architecture with a system comprising programmer, test designer, and test executor agents. These agents iteratively generate, test, and refine code, resulting in performance gains on Hu-manEval and MBPP benchmarks over state-of-the-art single-agent models. Similarly, CodeTree [34] introduces a tree-search-based planning strategy for LLM agents, which guides decision-making across code generation stages via both environment feedback and LLM-generated reasoning. Another approach, CodeCoR, integrates self-reflective evaluation into the multi-agent code pipeline, combining prompt design, test generation, repair, and re-coding steps to ensure robustness and quality of results [45]. These frameworks often feature feedback loops where code is revised based on syntactic or semantic failures, a technique that has also been extended to security-enhanced frameworks such as AutoSafeCoder, which includes fuzz testing and static analysis agents to enforce safety constraints [41]. INTERVENOR [59] employs a collaborative agent architecture consisting of a Code Learner and a Code Teacher. The Code Learner is responsible for generating an initial code implementation and compiling it to assess correctness. Upon detecting errors, the Code Teacher analyzes both the faulty code and the resulting error reports, then provides targeted repair instructions to guide the refinement of the solution. Self-repair [42] and TGen [40] improve code quality by leveraging feedback from the execution of predefined test cases to iteratively refine and correct generated programs. MAGIS [53] introduces a four-agent system (Manager, Custodian, Developer, and QA) that cooperatively resolves GitHub issues, achieving up to an eightfold increase in resolution rates compared to GPT-4 alone. AgentMesh [30] structures agents into Planner, Coder, Debugger, and Reviewer roles that sequentially transform high-level requirements into tested, validated code, a practical orchestration of developer workflows. Self-Organized Agents [27] propose a dynamically scaled agent group to collaboratively generate and optimize ultra-large codebases. Their framework surpasses single-agent baselines by 5% in pass@1 accuracy (i.e., the

While promising for software engineering tasks, multi-agent LLM systems also introduce new challenges in orchestration overhead, prompt alignment, tool integration, and reproducibility. Moreover, the environmental cost of multi-agent coordination remains largely unexplored. Increased agent interactions and tool calls often translate into higher inference latency and energy usage, raising important concerns around sustainability that existing frameworks do not address explicitly. 

2.3 Energy & Sustainability in LLMs 

The environmental footprint of modern AI has become a central research concern. Early landmark analyses quantified the energy and carbon costs of training large NLP models and called for standardized reporting practices [21, 52]. In parallel, the Green AI perspective argued for evaluating models not only by accuracy but also by efficiency metrics such as energy, emissions, and compute, to counter the escalating resource demands of state-of-the-art systems [49]. Subsequent systems-level work showed that lifecycle emissions depend strongly on model size, training/inference efficiency, datacenter efficiency (PUE), and the carbon intensity of the electricity mix, highlighting that location and infrastructure choices can change emissions by orders of magnitude [46]. Beyond training, inference is now a dominant and fast-growing driver of AI’s footprint due to widespread deployment of LLMs. Studies emphasize that prompt length, decoding strategy, and multi-turn/agentic interaction patterns materially increase energy use and latency, raising the need for efficiency-aware evaluation and reporting during inference as well as training [2, 10, 21, 25, 26, 46, 48]. Tooling has emerged to help practitioners measure and report runtime impacts, e.g., CarbonTracker [3] for training runs and epoch-level estimates, MLCO2/CodeCarbon-style approaches [32] for standardized emissions estimation from energy data and regional grids, and MELODI [26] specialized for measuring energy consumption of LLM inference. A complementary line of work targets algorithmic and systems optimizations to reduce inference cost: low-bit quantization and efficient kernels (e.g., GPTQ [16] and LLM.int8 [13]), context and routing strategies that avoid unnecessary large-model calls (e.g., FrugalGPT [7]), and faster decod-ing methods such as speculative decoding and multi-draft generation that cut token-generation latency and compute [20, 33]. Hardware-aware inference optimizations—including fused, I/O-aware attention kernels, paged KV-cache management, and KV-cache compression or eviction—reduce memory traffic and improve inference throughput on GPUs [12, 31, 67]. However, their energy benefits depend strongly on the workload, software stack, and accelerator architecture [15]. Hard-ware–software co-design for edge inference can further improve energy efficiency, reinforcing the need to jointly tune models, workloads, runtimes, and hardware for sustainable deployment [23]. Finally, sustainability extends beyond carbon and electricity. Resource accounting frameworks increasingly include water footprint and thermal constraints for datacenters, with evidence that large-scale LLM training and inference can incur substantial hidden water usage depending on cooling strategies and geography [35]. Together, these strands motivate rigorous, multi-objective evaluation for accuracy and efficiency (energy, CO 2, latency), and transparent reporting standards, especially for agentic LLM workflows where longer contexts and multi-turn exchanges can amplify resource consumption. 

2.4 Gaps in the Literature 

While the prior sections document advances in multi-agent LLM systems within software engi-neering and the growing attention to environmental implications of AI, three major gaps remain:

• Lack of Comprehensive Empirical Evaluations Across Agentic Configurations. Most existing studies primarily evaluate their systems on isolated tasks (e.g., code generation or issue resolution), often comparing single-agent baselines to a single agentic design [18]. However, there is a lack of systematic empirical work that evaluates multiple agentic con-figurations (e.g., non-agentic, single-agent, dual-agent, multi-agent workflows) across a diverse set of software engineering tasks. This makes it difficult to generalize findings across development activities in the software lifecycle. 

• Missing Joint Evaluation of Sustainability and Performance Metrics. The growing corpus on Green AI and AI’s environmental impact emphasizes energy and CO 2 metrics alongside accuracy (e.g., [10, 21, 26, 46, 48, 49, 52]). However, no prior work in SE-focused multi-agent LLM systems has simultaneously measured accuracy, inference latency, energy consumption, and estimated carbon emissions for different architectural configurations. This omission hinders our ability to understand the trade-offs between performance and sustainability when deploying agentic systems. 

• Reevaluating the Need for Agentic Complexity. Recent work such as that by Xia et al. [62] questions whether elaborate agent architectures are always necessary. The authors show that a simpler, non-agentic three-phase approach (localization, repair, patch validation) can outperform complex agents in both performance and cost metrics on SWE-bench Lite benchmarks. This suggests that agentic workflows may not always be the most efficient solution, and emphasizes the need for systematic comparison across configurations with sustainability in mind. By explicitly addressing these gaps (multi-configuration comparison across tasks, integrated performance-sustainability measurement, and validation of agent simplicity), our study fills a critical void in both the SE and AI sustainability literature.

3.2.2 Technical Debt Detection. We used an industry-relevant code smell dataset called MLCQ 

(Madeyski Lewowski Code Quest) [38]. It contains nearly 15,000 code snippets in Java. Each snippet was independently reviewed by experts who were asked to indicate the presence or absence of a specific smell type. Two of these smell types are function-level ( Long Method , Feature Envy ). The other two are class-level ( Blob , Data Class ). Every code snippet that is checked for one of these smell types is labeled with one of the severity levels: None , Minor , Major , Critical . A single code snippet could be checked for multiple smells by the same or different experts. We analyzed recurring code snippets across different samples to consolidate a dataset for our evaluation. For the same code snippet with multiple reviews at either the function or class level: 

• If all reviews label the snippet with severity None , we also assign it the None label, indicating that it is not associated with any smell. 

• If multiple smells are reported with severity other than None , we exclude the snippet to avoid ambiguity, since the assigned task is to identify whether any smell was present and, if so, which one. 

• If the severity for one review is None and it is Major or Critical for the other type of smell at the corresponding level, we assign its label for the associated smell. For example, if the review for Long Method indicates severity None , but the review for Feature Envy indicates severity Critical , then we assign the label Feature Envy with severity Critical to that code snippet. 

• We discarded entries labeled with Minor severity, as well as those containing code snippets from inaccessible repositories, missing files, or outdated or hidden content. As a result, we obtained a dataset with 6,441 samples without repeated entries (reviews) for any code snippet associated with one of the various types of smells. However, this dataset was still subject to code snippets that are possibly associated with multiple smell types at the same time. Reviewers assessing class-level code smells focused solely on class-level smell types [38]. However, function-level smells can also exist within these classes. To avoid ambiguity and ensure that each code snippet is associated with a single smell type, we manually checked all the class-level code snippets. If a class-level code snippet contained a function-level smell, we excluded it from the dataset. For our evaluation, we selected a reduced sample of 385 code snippets as summarized in Table 2. These samples are randomly shuffled.

3.4 Implementation Details 

We evaluated the models using Ollama [39], with each model configured using Ollama’s default context length (4096). To improve reproducibility and reduce output variability, all experiments were conducted with the temperature fixed at 0. Agent workflows were implemented using the AG2 framework (formerly AutoGen) 3, an open-source platform for building multi-agent systems. For prompt design, we adopted two strategies: zero-shot and few-shot prompting. In zero-shot prompting, the model receives only the task description, contextual information, and constraints, relying entirely on its pretrained knowledge. In few-shot prompting, the prompt additionally includes three manually selected labeled examples designed to capture diverse task-specific patterns (e.g., log event types, code smells, or code segments) to guide model behavior. These examples were not taken from the evaluation datasets to avoid data leakage. All prompt templates can be found in the replication package (https://github.com/merveast/agent-green).

3.7 Energy Measurement 

We used CodeCarbon [11] as the tool for monitoring energy consumption during all experimental runs. CodeCarbon provides an end-to-end framework for collecting hardware-level power data and attributing it to a given process. It combines a power estimation model for RAM, Running Average Power Limit (RAPL) for CPU, and NVIDIA Management Library (NVML) for GPU. CodeCarbon was selected for its ease of integration and ability to provide reproducible, component-level energy estimates without requiring external sensors. All experiments were run on the hardware setups described previously. Table 6 gives an overview of our energy measurement setup. CodeCarbon also estimates carbon emissions based on the energy consumption and carbon intensity of the energy grid in the geographic location where the experiments are run. We tracked carbon emissions during experiments using the offline mode of CodeCarbon, which is designed for environments without internet access. This mode preserves the same internal computation methodology but requires specifying a country_iso_code to approximate the carbon intensity of the electricity used. Since our computing resources were distributed across multiple geographic locations, we set the country code to CAN (Canada) as a representative average to ensure consistency across platforms. Canada’s grid carbon intensity, as estimated by CodeCarbon, is lower than the global average and substantially lower than grids powered predominantly by fossil fuels; the CO 2 figures reported here should therefore be interpreted as conservative lower-bound estimates, and comparisons across studies should account for location-specific grid intensity. Since CO 2

emissions in this case are a linear, constant-factor transformation of energy consumption, it adds no information beyond the energy measurements and are left out of the results in Section 4. We set CodeCarbon’s tracking_mode to "process," which estimates the power attributable specif-ically to the tracked process rather than the full-system ("machine") consumption. In this mode, CodeCarbon isolates the incremental resource usage (CPU via RAPL and GPU via pynvml) asso-ciated with the experiment, rather than reporting total node-level power draw. As a result, the reported energy does not correspond to the full system’s idle or baseline power consumption (e.g., the substantial idle draw of an 8×H100 server). Instead, it reflects the estimated share of power usage attributable to the active processes under measurement. Consequently, no explicit subtraction of an idle baseline was performed, since the methodology inherently aims to exclude non-utilized resources from the accounting. While this may underestimate total node-level energy, it provides a consistent basis for comparing experiments.

• Agent orchestration overhead (communication between agents, planning steps, and tool invocation),

3.9 Variables 

In our experiment, we analyzed the energy efficiency, inference performance, and output accuracy of different LLM system configurations across multiple software engineering tasks, LLM models, and hardware platforms. These aspects are influenced by various factors, which can be categorized as independent and dependent variables. 

3.9.1 Independent Variables (Factors We Manipulate). These are the variables we control or modify to observe their effects on the dependent variables: (1) Agent configuration – The architectural setup of the LLM system, including Non-Agentic (NA), Single-Agent (SA), Dual-Agent (DA), and Multi-Agent (MA) workflows. (2) Software engineering task – The specific task being performed: code generation, technical debt identification, code vulnerability detection, log parsing, or log analysis. (3) LLM model - The underlying large language model, varying across family, size, and special-ization (general-purpose vs. code-oriented models). (4) Hardware platform – The execution environment: Server-SMU, Workstation-SMU, and Server-STF. (5) Prompting strategy – The method of interacting with the LLM (zero-shot or few-shot prompting). 

3.9.2 Dependent Variables (Measured Outcomes). These are the variables we observe and measure to evaluate the impact of the independent variables: (1) Energy Consumption – Process-level energy consumed during execution, measured in Joules or kWh. (2) Inference latency – End-to-end execution time from task submission to final response generation, including model inference and agent coordination overhead. (3) Output Accuracy – Task-specific correctness metrics including Pass@1, parsing accuracy, edit similarity, LCS similarity, accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Macro F1, and per-class F1, depending on the task. By analyzing the impact of independent variables, we aimed to gain insights into how different design choices in LLM-based system configurations, task types, model selection, hardware platforms, and prompting strategies influence the trade-offs between accuracy, efficiency, and sustainability in software engineering applications. 

3.10 Experiment Execution 

To ensure that our measurements of energy consumption, latency, and accuracy were both con-sistent and reproducible, we implemented a strict and repeatable experiment execution protocol. Each experimental trial followed the same initialization, execution, and termination sequence to minimize uncontrolled variability and prevent cross-run interference. Figure 3 illustrates the overall experimental workflow. 

• Model Initialization Control: For each experimental run, the Ollama server was explicitly started immediately before execution and terminated immediately afterward. This ensured that each run began with a cold start, preventing the reuse of a model already loaded into memory. This step is essential because keeping a model resident in memory can artificially reduce both loading time and initial energy draw, thus biasing the measurements. By forcing the model to be loaded fresh for every run, we accurately captured the true energy cost and latency associated with model initialization. Additionally, Ollama’s keep_alive parameter was set to −1 to keep the model loaded in memory throughout the duration of each run.

• Consistent Input and Execution Parameters: All prompts, datasets, and execution pa-rameters were kept identical across repetitions for a given experimental condition. The temperature was fixed at 0 for all experiments to reduce output variability. Random seeds were fixed where applicable (e.g., in stochastic decoding strategies such as sampling) to reduce nondeterministic effects while still preserving the typical behavior of the LLM. By adhering to this controlled procedure, we ensured that observed differences in energy con-sumption, latency, or accuracy could be confidently attributed to changes in the independent variables (e.g., system configuration, LLM model, hardware platform) rather than to measurement noise or uncontrolled environmental factors.

Data Post-processing. The first step involves normalizing and cleaning the raw records. Column values for agent design, model name, hardware platform, and prompt type are standardized to canonical forms (e.g., consistent casing, whitespace trimming, and removal of timestamps from model identifiers). The cleaned run-level data is then aggregated into configuration-level summaries by grouping on the five configuration dimensions (task, agent, model, hardware, prompt) and computing the mean and standard deviation of each dependent variable across the repeated runs. This yields 640 unique configuration-level records, each summarizing up to three replicate runs. 

Visualization. We employed three visualization techniques to represent our analysis: (1) Box plots: To visualize the distribution of energy consumption metrics across different models and agentic configurations. Each box plot depicts the median (horizontal line splitting the main box), interquartile range (IQR, main box), and whiskers extending to 1.5 times the IQR, effectively summarizing central tendency and variability. (2) Scatter plots: To examine relationships between energy consumption and accuracy, with points representing different model configurations. Data points are colored and styled ac-cording to which model and dataset they represent. For certain figures, we plot the fitted regression lines, indicating trend lines. (3) Pareto frontier curves: Connecting Pareto-optimal configurations to visualize the trade-off boundary between energy efficiency and accuracy. 

3.12 Replication Package 

To support independent replication and extension of this study, we provide a replication package at https://github.com/merveast/agent-green. The package contains the source code used to implement the non-agentic, single-agent, dual-agent, and multi-agent configurations; the task-specific agent roles and interaction workflows; and the zero-shot and few-shot prompt templates. It also includes the scripts used to prepare the evaluation subsets from HumanEval, MLCQ, PrimeVul Pair, and HDFS-v1, together with the sampled instance identifiers and preprocessing rules needed to reconstruct the experimental datasets. The package further provides model and Ollama configurations, AG2 workflow definitions, CodeCarbon instrumentation, hardware and software environment specifications, and scripts for executing the experiments and collecting task outputs, latency, and component-level energy measurements. Aggregated per-configuration results are provided in a structured spreadsheet, together with the scripts used to calculate task-specific accuracy metrics, descriptive statistics, statistical tests, effect sizes, and Pareto fronts are included to enable verification of the reported findings. For models or datasets that cannot be redistributed directly because of licensing or size restrictions, the package provides acquisition instructions, version identifiers, and preprocessing scripts rather than copies of the original artifacts. A detailed README documents environment setup, dependency installation, experiment execu-tion, expected directory structure, and result reproduction. Machine-readable configuration files record the model, prompt strategy, agent configuration, task, hardware platform, and repetition associated with each run. Because absolute energy and latency measurements depend on the deploy-ment environment, exact numerical reproduction may require comparable hardware; nevertheless, the supplied artifacts support replication of the experimental procedure and evaluation pipeline on alternative platforms.

4 Experiment and Results 

This section discusses the results of our case studies, addressing each RQ in turn. Each subsection reports the effect of the four main factors (agent configuration, hardware platform, prompt strategy, and LLM model) in the same order, followed where applicable by task-specific details. 

Scope of cross-hardware aggregates. The GPT-OSS model was evaluated on a single hardware platform (Server-SMU) and without repeated runs due to its 20-billion-parameter footprint. To avoid biasing cross-hardware comparisons, all hardware-balanced aggregates exclude GPT-OSS unless explicitly noted. Model-level aggregates that span all hardware platforms include GPT-OSS only on Server-SMU; we flag the resulting interpretation caveats where they apply. 

4.1 Effectiveness of Configurations (RQ1) 

RQ1 investigates how the LLM system configurations perform across the five software engineering tasks, by reporting task-specific performance metrics for each task: Pass@1 for code generation, Macro F1 for technical debt detection, F1-score for vulnerability detection and log analysis, and parsing accuracy for log parsing. 

4.1.1 Effect of agentic configuration. Figure 4 summarizes the average performance of the four configurations. The main pattern is that increasing agentic complexity does not systematically improve accuracy, but the vulnerability-detection results provide an important exception. The non-agentic baseline (NA) obtains the highest mean score for code generation, technical debt detection, log parsing, and log analysis, whereas MA obtains the highest mean score for vulnerability detection. For code generation, all configurations perform strongly, with Pass@1 ranging from 94.2% for SA to 96.8% for NA. This suggests that, for the HumanEval-style tasks considered here, additional agentic orchestration is not necessary to achieve high functional correctness. Technical debt detection shows a similarly flat pattern across configurations, with Macro F1 between 40.0% and 42.8%. The low absolute scores indicate that the task is challenging, but the differences between agentic designs are small. Vulnerability detection is the clearest exception to the overall pattern. MA obtains the highest average F1-score (55.4%), followed by NA (49.7%) and SA (49.2%), while DA remains substantially lower (35.6%). Thus, the multi-agent workflow appears beneficial for this task, but the benefit is specific to MA rather than to agentic complexity in general.

RQ1 Conclusion. More complex agentic configurations do not provide a general accuracy benefit across the evaluated software engineering tasks, but MA is a task-specific exception for vulnerability detection. NA or SA is sufficient, and often best, for most tasks, while DA remains weak for vulnerability detection and the log tasks. The strongest effectiveness drivers are task-specific: prompt examples are crucial for log parsing, model choice varies sharply by task, and technical debt detection requires class-level analysis because Feature Envy remains difficult even when aggregate Macro F1 appears moderate. Hardware has a negligible effect on accuracy, and the hardware-balanced statistical analysis supports this descriptive pattern: agentic configuration has a significant accuracy effect only for vulnerability detection.

4.2.2 Effect of hardware platform. Hardware affects energy consumption, but the effect depends strongly on the agentic configuration, as summarized in Table 16. For lightweight configurations, the differences between platforms are relatively small: NA ranges from 0.082 kWh on Workstation-SMU to 0.162 kWh on Server-SMU, and SA ranges from 0.120 kWh on Workstation-SMU to 0.173 kWh on Server-STF. Hardware comparisons are most important when evaluating more complex agentic workflows; for lightweight configurations, architectural and model choices often dominate. The composition of that energy also differs markedly across platforms, as shown in Figure 8. On Server-SMU, energy is almost entirely GPU-bound (97%), with negligible CPU and RAM contributions. The relative utilization of compute components is therefore hardware-specific rather than fixed, which reinforces that hardware cannot be modeled as a single constant multiplier on a GPU-only cost.

4.3 Inference Latency Comparison (RQ3) 

RQ3 examines the end-to-end inference latency of the evaluated configurations. Latency includes the complete time from issuing the prompt to receiving the final output, including model inference and any agent orchestration overhead. As in RQ2, the tables report mean ± standard deviation across repeated runs for each configuration. 

4.3.1 Effect of agentic configuration. Table 19 presents the latency for each task–hardware pair across the four agentic configurations, while Table 20 summarizes the corresponding hardware-level and overall averages. The overall pattern closely mirrors the energy results in RQ2: latency increases with agentic complexity, but the magnitude is task-dependent. slower than NA, DA is 3.32 × slower, and MA is 6.07 × slower. These exceptions suggest that single-agent orchestration can occasionally streamline generation by producing more focused outputs.

4.3.5 Energy–latency relationship. A distinctive feature of RQ3 is the near-perfect correlation between energy and latency. Across all hardware-balanced configurations, the Pearson correlation between log-transformed energy and log-transformed latency is 𝑟 = 0.987 ( 𝑝 < 10 −100 ), as shown in Figure 12. This strong log–log relationship implies that configurations which are energy-expensive are also slow, and vice versa. In practical terms, this means that energy savings and latency savings are largely aligned: a configuration change that reduces one will almost always reduce the other. This tight coupling also means that the latency table can serve as a proxy for relative energy comparisons when energy measurements are unavailable.

5 Discussion 

This section synthesizes the findings of RQ1–RQ4 across tasks, configurations, models, and hardware platforms, and turns them into actionable design guidelines for sustainable LLM-based developer tools. Finally, we discuss the practical implications for tool builders and researchers. 

5.1 Insights across RQs 

Four patterns emerge consistently across the four research questions. Together they reframe the role of each design lever in agentic LLM systems for software engineering. 

Agentic configuration is a first-order factor, but its effect is asymmetric. Agentic configuration is one of the largest single drivers of system behaviour in our study, but its effect on accuracy is not monotonic. On accuracy, NA obtains the highest mean score for four of the five tasks, while MA is the strongest configuration for vulnerability detection. Adding agents otherwise leaves accuracy roughly unchanged (code generation, technical debt detection, log analysis) or degrades it (log parsing, and DA for vulnerability detection). On cost, the same axis grows steeply: averaged across tasks, hardware, models, and prompts, MA consumes 6.36 × as much energy and runs 6.07 × as long as NA, with worst-case slowdowns of up to 160 × for individual task–hardware pairs. The asymmetry is therefore that accuracy gains from additional agents are rare and task-specific, while the resource cost is systematic. The Pareto analysis in RQ4 makes this concrete, showing that out of 66 Pareto-optimal configurations across all task–hardware pairs, 36 are NA, 23 are SA, 6 are DA, and only 1 is MA. This does not mean that one should in general not use agents, but that there should be a clear justification for using them. Our findings complement the recent work of Tran and Kiela [56], who show that under matched reasoning-token budgets, single-agent systems consistently match or outperform multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning tasks. While their study focuses on reasoning under equal compute budgets and explains this behavior through an information-theoretic perspective, our results reveal a similar pattern across a broader range of software engineering tasks under realistic deployment conditions while explicitly quantifying the associated energy and latency costs. Across five software engineering tasks, additional agents provide only limited, task-specific accuracy improvements while imposing systematic increases in energy consumption and inference latency. The two studies therefore converge on the same practical conclusion from different perspectives: agentic complexity should not be treated as a default design choice but adopted only when there is clear evidence of a task-specific benefit.

Energy and latency move together; accuracy moves separately. 5.2 Design Guidelines for Sustainable Agentic LLM Systems 

Drawing on the insights above, we offer the following guidelines for practitioners building LLM-based developer tools. Each guideline is based on our RQ findings. 

G1: Start non-agentic, escalate only with evidence. For all five tasks studied, NA is the best or tied-for-best configuration on at least one of accuracy, energy, or latency, and it dominates the Pareto front (55% of all Pareto-optimal configurations). Adding agents should be treated as a hypothesis to test, and not a default. SA is a reasonable next step when a measurable task-specific benefit is observed; DA and MA should be reserved for tasks, such as vulnerability detection in our results, where there is concrete evidence that multi-round reasoning helps enough to justify the cost. 

G2: Match the model to the task before tuning the architecture. Model choice has a larger and more task-specific effect than agentic configuration. Table 13 can be used as a starting point: G3-4B for code generation, GPT-OSS or G3-4B for technical debt detection, Q3-4B for vulnerability detection, Q2.5C-7B for log tasks where accuracy is paramount, and CG-7B/Q3-4B when energy efficiency is important. The attractiveness of a model depends on whether it is sufficient for the task, and not on its parameter count alone: smaller is not automatically more efficient (e.g., Q2.5C-7B can consume more than two orders of magnitude more energy than the best model on log parsing). 

G3: Use few-shot prompting for output-structure-sensitive tasks. Few-shot prompting is the single most impactful lever for log parsing, raising parsing accuracy from 3.4% to 54.3% and reducing energy by 24–41%. It also reduces energy for technical debt detection. For code generation, however, few-shot examples can hurt both accuracy and energy. Few-shot prompting is therefore appropriate when the task is sensitive to output format, and should be tested empirically otherwise. 

G4: Choose hardware based on workload, not on raw capability. For lightweight (NA/SA) workloads, hardware differences are modest, and choices can be driven by availability or cost. For heavier (DA/MA) workloads, hardware becomes a first-order concern: high-end servers can be 3.4 × faster than workstations on MA, and the energy gap widens accordingly. If the workload must be agentic, invest in suitable hardware; otherwise, the savings from a simpler architecture exceed the savings from upgrading hardware. 

G5: Report energy alongside accuracy. The strong correlation between energy and latency (RQ3) and the dominance of lightweight configurations on the Pareto front (RQ4) only become visible because all four dimensions were measured uniformly. We encourage authors of new agentic systems to publish accuracy together with energy on a fixed reference platform, so that the community can compare designs on a more complete basis than accuracy alone. 

5.3 Implications 

For tool builders. The strongest practical takeaway is that competitive developer tools can be built around lightweight LLM configurations, provided that the model and prompt are matched to the task. Multi-agent orchestration should be added selectively, instrumented with energy or latency reporting, and validated against a non-agentic baseline before being shipped to users. 

For researchers. Many existing comparisons of agentic systems report only accuracy and only on a single task. Our results show that conclusions drawn from such single-axis evaluations may be misleading: configurations that look attractive on accuracy alone (e.g., MA on vulnerability detection) can be substantially more expensive than lower-cost alternatives, while configurations  

> , Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. Engineering Sustainable Agents: A Systematic Comparison of Agentic LLMs for Developer Workflows 43

that look attractive on cost alone may be insufficient for tasks where the accuracy gap matters. We advocate for multi-objective, multi-task evaluation as a standard practice for agentic LLM research. 

For sustainability assessment. The energy footprint of agentic LLM systems is not determined by any single design choice, but by the multiplicative interaction of model, agent configuration, prompt, and hardware. Across our 1,840 experimental runs, the campaign consumed approxi-mately 651.7 kWh of electricity, which CodeCarbon translates to roughly 110.8 kg CO 2e at the default carbon intensity used in our experiments (mean implied intensity 0.170 kg CO 2e/kWh). The absolute number is modest—comparable to a few hundred kilometres of driving an average passenger car [58]—but it is dominated by a small subset of configurations: the most expensive single configuration alone accounts for about 5.5 kg CO 2e, while the median configuration is roughly two orders of magnitude lower. This means that emissions reduction in agentic LLM workflows is not a question of small efficiency gains across the board, but more about avoiding the worst configurations. We therefore caution against treating CO 2 as a stand-alone indicator: under a fixed carbon-intensity assumption, it is a linear transformation of energy and adds no analytical insight beyond the energy results, but its translation into emissions depends strongly on when and 

where a model is run. Sustainability assessments of agentic systems should report energy at a fixed reference platform (so that designs can be compared) together with a transparent CO 2 estimate that makes the carbon-intensity assumption explicit, and they should highlight the long tail of high-cost configurations rather than the mean. 

6 Threats to Validity 6.1 Internal Validity 

Threats to internal validity concern whether the observed effects can be attributed to the experi-mental factors we studied. To counter such threats, we used fixed prompts and datasets, set the temperature to 0, disabled agent-level caching, restarted the Ollama server between runs, and executed repeated trials under the same protocol. Still, some sources of stochasticity may remain, such as low-level nondeterminism due to GPU kernels, model-serving behavior, and hardware state. Our three repeated runs per configuration (except for GPT-OSS) capture some variability, but do not fully represent long-term variation due to system temperature, background services, or hardware aging. Results involving GPT-OSS should therefore be interpreted with additional caution, since this model was only evaluated on one hardware platform and without repeated runs. To reduce the risk that hardware constraints affected the accuracy analysis, we have done balanced hardware comparisons for RQ1 by averaging the numbers across hardware platforms before averaging across experimentation configurations, preventing that the models run on multiple hardware platforms are weighted more than the GPT-OSS model in the reported numbers. 

6.2 External Validity 

External validity concerns the generalizability of our findings. We evaluate five different software engineering tasks, which gives a broader coverage than single-task benchmarks, but it is still only a subset of developer workflows. The results may not generalize to tasks such as code review, test generation, repository-level maintenance, or autonomous development. The generalizability is also restricted by the datasets, since real-life use cases generally involve more complex, noisy data and larger inputs compared to the curated benchmark datasets used to evaluate each configuration in this study. Additionally, the datasets used in this study cover a limited set of programming languages: HumanEval targets Python, and the MLCQ and PrimeVul datasets consist of Java and C/C++ code, respectively. The findings on code generation, technical debt detection, and  

> , Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. 44 Astekin et al.

vulnerability detection may therefore not generalize to other languages, particularly those with different syntactic structures, ecosystem conventions, or levels of LLM training coverage. The model and hardware choices also present a threat to external validity, as we have evaluated only small, local LLMs with a maximum of 20 billion parameters. Results may differ for larger models, and particularly frontier models. Publicly hosted frontier models, however, do not allow direct local measurement of energy consumption, which was one of the main aims of this study. Similarly, cloud deployments may behave differently because of batching, virtualization, datacenter power usage effectiveness, cooling infrastructure, and location-specific carbon intensity. Our results are affected by our specific implementation of the agentic configurations and the prompts used for the various tasks. Agentic systems can be designed in a multitude of ways, and small details can create significant changes. For simpler, more structured tasks such as log parsing and technical debt detection, we adopted generic orchestration patterns (Actor–Critic and User Proxy–Actor–Critic–Refiner) that are broadly applicable across tasks. For tasks requiring deeper domain reasoning (vulnerability detection, log analysis, and code generation), we defined task-specific agent roles informed by prior literature, allowing us to evaluate representative and validated multi-agent setups rather than purely generic ones. More specialized or extensively tuned multi-agent designs could yield different accuracy results for individual tasks, particularly for vulnerability detection where our MA configuration already shows a meaningful accuracy advantage. Our claim is therefore not that the specific configurations studied are optimal, but that they represent realistic design choices a practitioner would make. The finding that lightweight configurations dominate the Pareto front should be read as evidence about the cost–benefit ratio of typical agentic orchestration, not as a proof that no multi-agent design can ever achieve a better accuracy–energy trade-off. Future work could explore the multi-agent design space for individual tasks to identify whether more task-specialized orchestration can shift the Pareto frontier. 

6.3 Construct Validity 

Construct validity concerns whether the measurements used in the study capture the concepts we intend to analyze. For task performance, we use the term accuracy as an umbrella term, while the actual metric is task-specific: Pass@1 for code generation, Macro F1 and per-class F1 for technical debt detection, F1-score for vulnerability detection and log analysis, and parsing accuracy for log parsing. This improves the fit between metric and task, but it also means that performance values are not directly interchangeable across tasks. Cross-task averages should therefore be interpreted as summaries of relative behavior across the benchmark suite, not as a single universal measure of software engineering capability. The technical debt task is a particular construct-validity risk because plain accuracy can hide poor performance on minority smell classes. We mitigate this by using Macro F1 as the primary task metric and by reporting per-class F1 scores. However, these metrics still reduce a complex code-quality judgment to a fixed-label classification problem and do not capture severity, maintainability impact, or disagreement among human reviewers. Energy consumption and latency are also affected by specific measurement choices. CodeCarbon estimates process-level energy consumption from CPU, GPU, and memory signals rather than measuring wall-socket energy with external instrumentation. This provides a consistent basis for comparing configurations, but it may result in greater uncertainty, depending on the idle power draw on various hardware platforms. A further construct-validity consideration concerns the post-processing used to extract predic-tions from LLM outputs. Since LLMs generate free-form natural language responses, each task required a task-specific parser or extraction procedure to map the generated text to the expected evaluation format (e.g., labels, code, or structured log templates). To mitigate this issue, our prompts  

> , Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. Engineering Sustainable Agents: A Systematic Comparison of Agentic LLMs for Developer Workflows 45

explicitly instructed the models to produce responses in predefined structured formats. Neverthe-less, adherence to these formats varied across models, reflecting differences in response style and instruction-following behavior. Deviations from the expected format occurred in some cases, par-ticularly in multi-agent configurations where the final output resulted from multiple intermediate reasoning steps. Although the post-processing rules were applied consistently across all configura-tions for a given task, alternative extraction strategies or more robust output normalization could yield different measured accuracy, particularly when a response deviated from the expected format while still conveying the correct semantic answer. Consequently, the reported accuracy reflects both the underlying model performance and the effectiveness of the response extraction procedure adopted in this study. 

7 Conclusion 

This paper evaluated the effectiveness, energy consumption, and latency of agentic LLM configura-tions across five software engineering tasks, six LLMs, three hardware platforms, and two prompt strategies. The results show that increasing agentic complexity does not provide a general accuracy benefit. The multi-agent configuration improves average accuracy for vulnerability detection, but NA or SA is sufficient, and often strongest, for the other tasks. The sustainability cost of additional agents is much more systematic. Averaged across tasks, models, prompts, and hardware platforms, MA consumes 6.36 × as much energy and runs 6.07 × as long as NA, while appearing in only one of the 66 Pareto-optimal configurations. Energy and latency are also tightly coupled ( 𝑟 = 0.987 in log–log space), which means that inefficient configurations are usually both more energy-intensive and slower. Across the full experimental campaign, the runs consumed 651.7 kWh and corresponded to 110.8 kg CO 2e under the fixed carbon-intensity assumption used in the study. The main practical implication is that sustainable agentic LLM system design should start from a lightweight baseline and escalate only when a task-specific benefit is measured. Model and prompt selection often matter more than adding agents: model choice varies sharply by task, and few-shot prompting is especially important for structured-output tasks such as log parsing. Future evaluations of agentic software engineering systems should therefore report accuracy together with energy and latency, so that claimed improvements can be assessed as full cost–benefit trade-offs rather than accuracy gains in isolation. Beyond evaluation methodology, future work should investigate alternative role assignments and coordination strategies within multi-agent configurations to determine whether more task-specialized designs can shift the Pareto frontier without proportionally increasing energy and latency costs. Future work will also extend the evaluation to repository-scale and tool-augmented software engineering tasks, additional model families, and production deployment environments to assess the generalizability of the observed trade-offs. We will further investigate adaptive orchestration policies that activate additional agents only when task uncertainty or expected accuracy gains justify the energy and latency overhead.
