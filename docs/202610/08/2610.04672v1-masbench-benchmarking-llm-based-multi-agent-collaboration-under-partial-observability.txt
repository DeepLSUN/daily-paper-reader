## ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) have progressively evolved into the core of au-tonomous agents. Building on this progress, LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) coordinate multiple agents into a synergistic team to accomplish complex tasks that exceed the capabilities of individual agents. The effectiveness of such systems depends not only on the agents themselves, but also on how collabora-tion mechanisms are designed and organized. Note that real-world collaboration is typically partially observable , where each agent can only access partial in-formation about the environment due to physical or privacy-related constraints. However, many existing multi-agent benchmarks assume global observability, and leave limited support for systematically evaluating collaboration mechanisms. To bridge this gap, we introduce MASBench , a multi-agent collaboration benchmark designed under partially observable constraints. It is organized into three progres-sive task categories: Reasoning , Scheduling , and Game . Through this structure, we progressively evaluate three representative collaboration mechanisms: Proto-col , Memory , and Routing . MASBench further provides deterministic evaluation metrics, including performance score, communication cost, and communication efficiency, to characterize both collaboration outcomes and communication over-head. Experiments across diverse LLM backbones and mechanism configura-tions offer empirical guidance for effective MAS design. Code is available at: 

https://github.com/BUPT-GAMMA/MASBench .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model-based (LLM-based) agents can decompose complex objectives into executable steps, invoke external tools, and perform iterative corrections based on environmental feedback, laying the foundation for complex planning and long-horizon task execution Yao et al. (2022); Schick et al. (2023); Shinn et al. (2023). Building upon this, multi-agent systems (MAS) Hong et al. (2023); Li et al. (2023); Xi et al. (2025) organize multiple autonomous agents via collaboration mechanisms Packer et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2023b); Marro et al. (2024); Liang et al. (2024); Shen et al. (2025), enabling them to work synergistically as a team. This paradigm significantly expands the boundaries of agent applications, handling intricate scenarios ranging from software development Hong et al. (2023); Qian et al. (2024) to strategic simulations FAIR et al. (2022); Park et al. (2023); Xu et al. (2023). It is worth emphasizing that the performance gains in multi-agent systems do not merely stem from increasing the number of agents but fundamentally depend on the design and organization of collabo-ration mechanisms Li et al. (2024); Gridach et al. (2025). Synthesizing existing literature and practice, here we focus on three representative mechanisms: memory improves long-term collaboration by recording, summarizing, and maintaining key information across multi-turn interactions; routing 

facilitates efficient information exchange by determining the communication topology; and protocol 

organizes communication through message schemas and interaction rules.

Scheduling (Focus on Memory & Protocol): This category simulates privacy-restricted meeting scheduling. Each agent can only access its own schedule and is prohibited from publicly disclosing the complete calendar. Agents must discuss candidate time slots and verify feasibility via negotiation. 

Game (Focus on Memory, Routing & Protocol): This category describes a cooperative grid-world task, where multiple agents are required to collect and transport different types of resources under local observation. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 LLM-B ASED MAS LLM-based multi-agent systems have demonstrated remarkable effectiveness in tackling complex tasks Li et al. (2023; 2024); Guo et al. (2024). Prior studies suggest MAS effectiveness is strongly influenced by collaboration mechanisms : Memory maintains key state across turns Packer et al. (2023); Park et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2024); Zhong et al. (2024). For example, Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) utilizes memory streams to maintain behavioral consistency. Routing 

shapes information flow by deciding who communicates with whom Liu et al. (2023b); Wu et al. (2024); Zhuge et al. (2024); Shen et al. (2025). For example, DyLAN (Liu et al., 2023b) dynamically optimizes interaction graphs based on context. Protocol standardizes interaction via structured stages and message schemas Song et al. (2023); Chen et al. (2025a); Marro et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025b). For example, OPTIMA explicitly optimizes inter-agent communication by training on trajectories that jointly reward task performance and token efficiency (Chen et al., 2025b). Beyond these mechanisms, the community has explored decision-optimization strategies, such as multi-agent debate (Liang et al., 2024), reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), and voting (Wang et al., 2023) to enhance decision quality. 2.2 BENCHMARKS FOR LLM-BASED MAS Prior studies often adopt general-purpose benchmarks, emphasizing outcome-oriented task completion under relatively loose interaction conditions ( e.g., GAIA, AgentBench) (Liu et al., 2023a; Mialon et al., 2023). With the rise of LLM-based multi-agent systems, benchmarks have expanded toward collaborative task solving (Zhu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025) and simulation (Zhou et al., 2023). Recent benchmarks further introduce partially observable settings (Zhou et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Ruan et al., 2025). Despite these advances, existing benchmarks are primarily designed for end-to-end agent capability evaluation, rather than controlled analysis of how different collaboration mechanisms contribute under partial observability. Moreover, many multi-agent benchmarks rely on LLM-as-a-judge for at least part of their assessment, which is prompt-sensitive and vulnerable to systematic biases, undermining reproducibility (Zheng et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025). By contrast, MASBench focuses on mechanism-level evaluation: it organizes tasks to progressively stress protocol, memory, and routing, and reports deterministic task-grounded performance, communication cost and communication efficiency. Besides, there are also domain-specific multi-agent benchmarks that simulate structured role interac-tions in realistic workflows (Dong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Long et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2024). These benchmarks are complementary to MASBench, but they typically prioritize environment-specific adaptation of individual agents rather than assessing collaboration mechanisms. 

## 3 PRELIMINARY 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

In this paper, we formalize the multi-agent system as a tuple ⟨G , A, E⟩ , where G is the overall goal, 

A is a set of LLM-based agents, and E is the shared environment. Now we describe the process of a multi-round collaboration. Specifically, each agent Ai ∈ A is defined as a tuple ⟨O i, m i, Mi, Di, Ci⟩:• Oi is the observation function that perceives the current environment and receives messages sent to Ai in the last round; • mi is the memory of agent Ai;• Mi is the memory management function that updates memory based on the current observation as well as Ai’s memory and actions from last round; • Di is the decision function that outputs the action interacting with the environment based on the current memory and observations; • Ci is the communication function that outputs the action interacting with other agents based on the current memory and observations. The global state of environment E will transit given the actions of all agents. Synthesizing existing literature and practice Packer et al. (2023); Shen et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2023b); Marro et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025a), we focus on three key mechanisms that regulate the collaboration process: 

Memory. The memory mechanism supports the consistency of long-term collaboration. By dynami-cally recording and summarizing past observations and interactions, memory maintains a historical representation to assist an agent’s decision making. 

Routing. The routing mechanism determines the communication topology, namely who communi-cates with whom and when . By dynamically selecting the most suitable agent to communicate with, routing can facilitate more effective and timely information exchange among the agents. 

Protocol. The protocol mechanism regulates how agent communication and interaction are organized. By specifying message schemas and interaction rules, protocols can help reduce interaction ambiguity and promote the stability of the collaboration process. Routing and protocol primarily regulate the communication function, while task-specific interaction rules may also constrain or inform the decision function. 

## 4 MASB ENCH 

4.1 BENCHMARK OVERVIEW 

As shown in Figure 2, MASBench provides a comprehensive evaluation framework for multi-agent collaboration under partial observability. We adopt a mechanism-driven perspective to evaluate how agents leverage memory, protocols, and routing to bridge the information gaps from partial observability. MASBench comprises three tasks (reasoning, scheduling, and game), designed to progressively increase collaboration mechanism demands. 4Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 4.2 BENCHMARK DESIGN 

The statistics of the three tasks are shown in Table 1. Each of the tasks targets on the evaluation of specific mechanisms. 

Reasoning. Reasoning provides the most compact collaboration setting, where two agents are required to answer a question based on the distinct portions of information assigned to each. Specif-ically, We adapt multi-hop instances from HotpotQA Yang et al. (2018) to form a collaborative inference task under partial observability. For each problem, the evidence chain is decomposed and distributed across two agents, so that no single agent has sufficient information to solve the task independently. Agents must exchange key clues and reach a final answer through multi-turn dialogue. We split instances into Simple and Complex subsets based on supporting-fact count, context length, and answer length. The detailed information are provided in Appendix D.1. Scheduling. Scheduling task simulates aprivacy-constrained meeting coordination sce-nario, where multiple agents need to negotiate a meeting time feasible for everyone. Compared with Reasoning, each agent faces longer private inputs and more complex multi-round negotia-tion, making memory needed to be considered in this task. Since communication follows a broadcast-style chat, routing is not included. Specifically, Calendars are randomly generated with 10-minute granularity, and the group must agree on a continuous 20-minute meeting window. We quantify difficulty by the number of feasible 20-minute windows Nsolution : Simple instances have Nsolution ∈ { 4, 5}, while Complex instances have Nsolution ∈ { 2, 3}. The detailed calendar generation procedure is provided in Appendix D.2. 

Game. Game poses the richest collaboration demands in the benchmark as a cooperative Dec-POMDP-style grid-world task that requires spatio-temporal coordination under partial observability. Multiple agents must explore the map, collect resources, and deposit them into warehouses to earn scores within limited rounds. Since each agent only observes a local window and communication is point-to-point, all three mechanisms ( i.e., memory, protocol and routing) are crucial. The environment contains terrain cells, resources, and warehouses with resource-specific quotas. Each agent observes a limited local window centered on itself. At each step, an agent takes an action to interact with the environment, including movement and item operations. Notably, each agent can carry only one type of resource at a time, subject to a maximum capacity. After that, agents may send point-to-point messages to interact with other agents. The messages will be received in the beginning of next round. Detailed action rules and scoring constraints are provided in Appendix D.3. Problem complexity is primarily controlled by map scale: Simple instances use 15 × 15 maps, while Complex 

instances use 18 × 18 maps with more resources and higher coordination demands. 4.3 EVALUATION METRICS 

We evaluate the tasks with the following three metrics for task performance and communication cost. 

1) Performance Score. We report the average normalized score on a [0 , 100] scale across all test instances. The scoring criteria for each task are defined as follows: • Reasoning: We compute word-level F1 between predicted and ground-truth answers for each instance, and report the mean F1 scaled by 100 .• Scheduling: We use the success rate, where an instance is counted as successful only if the agents output a meeting window feasible for all agents. The rate is scaled by 100 .• Game: We normalize the final collected-resource score by the map-specific reference score and scale it by 100 , and report the average normalized game score. Note that the complex subset does not necessarily indicate a lower performance score, since the number of agents and available resources also increases.

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

LLM Backbones. We conduct evaluations on five LLM backbones covering diverse capability– cost regimes: Llama-3.1-8B Dubey et al. (2024), GPT-5-nano OpenAI (2026), Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite Comanici et al. (2025), DeepSeek-v3.2 Liu et al. (2025), and Qwen-Plus Alibaba Cloud (2026). Due to the higher long-horizon planning and action-validity requirements in S CHEDULING and G AME ,Llama-3.1-8B exhibits a high failure rate in these two categories. Thus, we include Llama-3.1-8B only in the R EASONING category. 

Mechanism Configurations. Our benchmark focuses on three representative coordination mech-anisms in LLM-based MAS: M EMORY , P ROTOCOL , and R OUTING . For each task category, we evaluate only the subset of mechanisms that is operationally meaningful given the specific interaction structure and constraints. Concrete operationalizations are task-dependent and specified accordingly for each task category below, with implementation details provided in Appendix E. 

Reasoning. BASE uses free-form dialogue, while P ROTOCOL structures the interaction using typed messages for information requests, evidence sharing, and final answers. 

Scheduling. BASE uses unconstrained natural-language negotiation based on their private schedule and the recent dialogue context. M EMORY maintains structured availability and negotiation states, 6Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 including candidate and rejected meeting slots across rounds. P ROTOCOL further organizes the interaction into a multi-phase protocol ( e.g. , coarse search → candidate proposal → voting phase). 

Game. BASE disables inter-agent communication, yielding a baseline driven solely by local obser-vations. M EMORY stores discovered world states from local observations and received messages. ROUTING enables point-to-point communication with two recipient-selection modes: R ANDOM 

samples a teammate, while G ATE first decides whether communication is needed and then selects the recipient. P ROTOCOL further structures transmitted messages using grounded task-relevant fields, including agent state, observations, and current plans. 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

Strong backbone capability is decisive for task utility. Across all three task categories, Qwen-Plus achieves the highest Score , outperforming the other backbones. This performance gap indicates that backbone capability remains a decisive factor for reaching higher task utility under partial observability, even when agents are equipped with collaboration mechanisms. Importantly, these results also demonstrate that MASBench can serve as a meaningful evaluation bedrock for backbone models themselves, rather than merely comparing coordination mechanisms. However, superior raw performance does not necessarily translate to better cost-effectiveness: al-though Qwen-Plus often leads on Score , it is frequently surpassed by other backbones on Eff ,suggesting that higher utility may come with disproportionate communication overhead. 

Mechanism gains are consistent across backbones and tasks. Across backbones, enabling task-relevant mechanisms generally improves collaboration effectiveness, indicating that the observed gains are not tied to a particular model. In Reasoning , P ROTOCOL improves coordination efficiency across backbones: Score increases modestly, ranging from +1 .5% to +28 .4% , while Tok drops substantially, by 45 .0% to 72 .7% ,resulting in a 2.0× to 4.2× gain in Eff .In Scheduling , M EMORY improves Score over B ASE , with a cross-round ablation (Appendix B.5) showing gains from state retention even without availability preprocessing. In this paper, we propose MASBench, a comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate multi-agent collaboration under partially observable constraints. ## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK ON LLM-BASED MULTI -A GENT SYSTEMS 

Existing MAS broadly fall into two categories. (i) General orchestration frameworks provide reusable abstractions for roles, interaction patterns, and communication topologies (Chen et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). For example, AgentVerse supports general-purpose task solving (Chen et al., 2024) with an iterative four-stage framework. (ii) Domain-specific frameworks customize roles and work-flows to meet the requirements of specific domains. In software engineering (Hong et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024), MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2023) and ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) decom-pose the development process into phased subtasks, where agents with distinct roles collaboratively perform analysis, design, implementation, and testing. In deep research (Boiko et al., 2023; Gridach et al., 2025; Ghafarollahi & Buehler, 2025), SciAgents leverages role-specialized agents, ontological knowledge graphs, and retrieval tools to propose and critique scientific hypotheses (Ghafarollahi & Buehler, 2025).

Reasoning. Alice and Bob receive the shared question and their respective private context blocks. Both framework implementations use the plain Base task prompt. Alice speaks first, and the agents alternate for at most ten individual turns, rather than ten pairs of turns. Execution stops at the first answer recognized by the benchmark parser, using an answer tag or a boxed answer, or at the turn limit if no answer is recognized. Conversation history is maintained by the framework throughout the run. The framework runs use temperature zero and a requested output limit of 4,096 tokens per turn. For GPT-5-nano, the adapter raises the output-token limit to 8,192 to accommodate reasoning-token usage. 

Scheduling. Each instance contains five members with private schedules over five days. The objective is to agree on a common 20-minute meeting window. The adapter deterministically converts each member’s schedule into maximal free intervals lasting at least 20 minutes and supplies these intervals only to the corresponding agent. This preprocessing replaces the reference implementation’s LLM-based availability extraction. The framework implementations use the plain task prompt and native conversation memory, without the reference’s explicit candidate-state controller or an additional team-memory summarizer. Each round consists of one utterance from each of the five members in sequence. After a complete round, 21 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 a separate stateless checker examines the public transcript for explicit agreement on a single slot. The checker has no access to private availability and does not propose meeting slots. The conversation ends when the checker reports completion or after five rounds. A deterministic evaluator checks the proposed window against all five private schedules. An infeasible proposal is scored as a failure even if the checker reports consensus; the evaluator does not repair the proposal. 

Game. Each framework agent interacts with the environment for 50 rounds, making one action call and one message-decision call per round. The message decision specifies whether to send, the eligible recipient, and the message content. Performance. Performance follows Sec. 4.3. For G AME , the raw resource score is normalized by the map-specific reference score before aggregation. Scores are scaled by 100 for reporting. Overall performance weights instances equally rather than averaging the Simple and Complex subset means with equal weight. For G AME , repetition-level results are averaged for each map before the map-level means are aggregated. 

Communication cost. Tok measures the total tokens generated for inter-agent messages, averaged over evaluation instances. Prompt tokens, private reasoning, private action-generation outputs, and non-message control outputs are excluded. D.1 REASONING 

D.1.1 DATA GENERATION BASED ON MULTI -HOP QUESTION ANSWERING 

We adapt multi-hop instances from HotpotQA Yang et al. (2018) to form a collaborative inference task under partial observability. For each problem, we decompose its evidence chain and distribute it across two agents so that no single agent has sufficient information to solve the task independently. D.1.2 QUALITY ASSURANCE AND SCORING 

We implement a multi-stage validation pipeline to filter low-quality generations. Each entry is assigned a composite score s ∈ [0 , 4] , defined as: 

s = sfmt + Iq + Isup + Iirr 

where I(·) denotes a binary indicator function. The components are defined as follows: • Format Check ( sfmt ): Validates JSON schema integrity and softly scores supporting-fact coverage. For each agent, the coverage score is the number of assigned supporting facts divided by 5 and capped at 1; sfmt is the average of the two agents’ scores. • Question Validity ( Iq): Verified via Gemini-Flash ; the question must be unanswerable without the provided context. • Sufficiency ( Isup ): The referenced supporting-fact sentences alone must be sufficient to recover the ground-truth answer. • Irrelevance ( Iirr ): After removing all supporting facts, the remaining context must be insufficient to recover the answer, ensuring no “shortcuts” exist. We employ a Retry-and-Select strategy, attempting generation up to 5 times per seed until a score (s > = 3 .5) is achieved. This LLM-assisted validation is used for dataset construction and filtering. D.1.3 DIFFICULTY STRATIFICATION

D.2 SCHEDULING 

To ensure the Scheduling benchmark serves as a rigorous testbed for multi-agent coordination, we implement a controllable generation pipeline that manages schedule density, solvability, and solution diversity. D.2.1 CALENDAR GENERATION 

The timeline is modeled as a set of discrete time units. Let m be the granularity parameter ( i.e., 

m = 10 minutes). The total time horizon is defined as T = D × 24 × 60 /m units, where D = 5 is the number of days. A valid solution is a continuous meeting window of duration δ = 20 minutes whose start and end times are aligned to the discretized timeline and feasible for all agents. 

Initialization and Fixed Constraints. For each agent, the schedule is initialized with fixed “Rest” blocks (e.g., sleep, meals) based on daily routines (e.g., Sleep: 00:00–07:00, Lunch: 12:00–14:00). These intervals are strictly marked as BUSY and removed from the available time slots Tavail .

Stochastic Busy Filling. To simulate realistic constraints, we fill the remaining non-fixed slots with random activities. A busy ratio ρi ∈ [0 .3, 0.6] is sampled for each agent. The generator iteratively samples a duration d ∈ [10 , 60] and an activity type (e.g., “study”, “work”) to insert a BUSY block, until the schedule density reaches ρi.24 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 After stochastic busy filling, we enumerate all common feasible meeting windows across agents. Instances with too few valid windows are discarded and regenerated, while instances with sufficient valid windows are further processed by candidate pruning. D.2.2 CANDIDATE SOLUTION PRUNING 

To strictly control the difficulty level, we implement a Candidate Pruning algorithm that controls the number of valid meeting windows. This ensures that the final dataset contains exactly K valid meeting slots specified by candidate num .Let V be the set of all valid meeting windows initially found in the generated schedules. If |V| < K ,the instance is discarded and regenerated. Otherwise, the algorithm proceeds as follows: 1. Protection Set ( P): We randomly sample K solutions from V to form the protected set 

P ⊂ V . These are the intended ground truths we wish to preserve. 2. Greedy Obstruction: We iteratively select a time point and an agent to insert a minimum-unit BUSY block. The goal is to invalidate non-protected solutions ( V \ P ) without affecting 

P.3. Termination: The process repeats until |V| = K. The schedules are then rewritten to include these artificial obstructions. D.2.3 DIFFICULTY STRATIFICATION 

After candidate pruning, the number of valid meeting windows K is used as the difficulty control variable. Instances with more feasible windows are considered easier because agents have more opportunities to reach a valid consensus. Accordingly, we define Simple instances with K ∈ { 4, 5}

and Complex instances with K ∈ { 2, 3}. This stratification controls negotiation difficulty while ensuring that every retained instance remains solvable. D.2.4 TASK SPECIFICATION AND EVALUATION

D.3.1 ENVIRONMENT 

The Game task is a cooperative resource-collection game in a partially observable 2D grid. Agents must discover resources, move them to compatible warehouses, and coordinate under a fixed episode budget. • Coordinates: The simulator uses Cartesian coordinates (x, y ), with x increasing from left to right and y increasing from bottom to top. The local ASCII view marks the acting agent with Y, other agents with a, resources with w/s/S , and warehouses with T.• Two-layer cells: Each cell has a terrain layer and an item layer. Terrain types include empty, mountain, river, wall, warehouse, forest, and grass. Mountains, rivers, and walls are impassable; warehouses are passable deposit tiles. The item layer stores stackable wood, sand, and stone resources. • Warehouses: Warehouses are deposit-only. Once resources are placed on a warehouse, they contribute to the team score and cannot be picked up again. Each warehouse has a total 26 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 capacity and per-resource capacity limits; deposited resources count toward the score only within these limits. • Partial observability: Each agent observes only a square local window centered on itself. Out-of-map cells are rendered as *. The current implementation does not use line-of-sight occlusion; visibility is determined by window distance. • Observation dictionary: The agent observation includes the current round, local ASCII view, global position, inventory status, current-tile information, visible resources, visible warehouses with remaining capacity, visible teammates, and messages delivered from the previous round. • Map families: Simple instances use 15 × 15 maps, while complex instances use 18 × 18 

maps with more agents and resources. Maps are manually specified and converted into machine-readable simulator files. D.3.2 OBJECTIVES AND SCORING

Score Normalization. Action Space. Agents output one discrete action per round: • Movement: UP , DOWN , LEFT , RIGHT , and STAY move to adjacent cells or remain in place. • Pickup: PICK UP [QTY] [TYPE] collects resources from the current tile only. An agent can carry multiple units but only one resource type at a time. • Deposit: PUT DOWN [QTY] deposits the carried resource on the current tile only. To score, the current tile must be a compatible warehouse with positive remaining capacity for the carried type. If an explicit quantity violates inventory, tile, or warehouse capacity constraints, the action fails rather than being silently repaired. 

Per-round Simulation Process. The current Game runtime uses a synchronous loop: 1. Messages generated in round t − 1 are delivered to their recipients. 2. Each agent receives its local observation and generates an action. 3. If communication is enabled, each agent may generate at most one point-to-point message using the same pre-update observation context. 4. The environment resolves movement conflicts, executes PICK UP /PUT DOWN , updates ware-house inventories and score, and advances the round counter. 

Repeated Evaluation. For each backbone and mechanism configuration, every map is executed five times. Performance and communication statistics are first averaged across the five runs for each map and then aggregated across maps. D.3.3 MAP EXAMPLES

## E MECHANISMS 

E.1 REASONING 

The Reasoning task uses a two-agent turn-based dialogue workflow under partial observability. We first describe the Base workflow, and then introduce the Protocol. E.1.1 BASE WORKFLOW 

The Base workflow defines the default interaction process for the Reasoning task and serves as the baseline setting without additional mechanisms. Each instance involves two agents, where each agent receives the question and a private subset of the evidence. Since neither agent has sufficient information to solve the task independently, agents must exchange information through a turn-based dialogue. 30 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • Observation: Each agent observes its private context, the question, and the full dialogue history generated so far. Each turn, the complete utterance of the current speaker is appended to a shared history visible to both participants. • Interaction: Communication is entirely in natural language. Agents may ask targeted ques-tions, share relevant evidence, and provide brief rationales when combining the partitioned evidence, without following a predefined message schema. • Termination: The system monitors the dialogue for specific termination triggers. A valid answer is identified by matching either the <A>answer</A> tags (taking the last occurrence) or the \boxed {answer } format (taking the first occurrence). E.1.2 PROTOCOL 

Protocol combines structured public messaging with evidence-seeking instructions and protocol-specific message extraction and termination. It preserves the private evidence partitions, speaker alternation, and task objective, while changing how information is requested, shared, and committed to a final answer. • Message Encapsulation: Agents distinguish their public message from internal reasoning using a designated MESSAGE block. Only the extracted public block is transmitted to the partner. • Functional Tagging: Within the <MESSAGE> block, agents must utilize structured tags to define their communicative intent: 

– <Q>...</Q> : To solicit specific missing information from the partner. 

– <R>...</R> : To provide evidence or responses to previously asked questions. 

– <A>...</A> : To propose a final answer. • Deterministic Termination: The dialogue terminates immediately upon the detection of an 

<A> tag within a <MESSAGE> block. If no answer is proposed within the max rounds (default 10), the session is marked as a failure. E.1.3 PROMPT TEMPLATES

E.2 SCHEDULING 

The Scheduling task models privacy-constrained group negotiation, where each agent only observes its own calendar and must coordinate with others to find a common meeting slot. We describe the Base workflow first, and then introduce Memory and Protocol. E.2.1 BASE WORKFLOW 

The Base workflow defines the default negotiation process and serves as the baseline setting without additional memory or protocol constraints. Agents negotiate in natural language based on their private schedules and the public conversation history. 32 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • Observation: Each agent receives its own private schedule, represented as a list of “Busy Activities” (e.g., 14:00-15:00 Meeting ). Agents must infer their free time by identifying gaps between these busy intervals. • Interaction: Communication follows a turn-based broadcast workflow. Agents speak sequentially, and each message is appended to a shared conversation history visible to all participants. • Termination: A designated agent acts solely as a neutral termination detector. It has no privileged access to private schedules or hidden agent states. Its only function is to analyze the public conversation log at the end of each round to determine if a consensus has been reached. E.2.2 MEMORY 

Memory further equips each agent with a precomputed availability whitelist by extracting feasible free intervals from its calendar, and accumulates candidate slots and rejected slots across rounds to reduce repeated reasoning. • Initialization: Before the negotiation begins, each agent’s calendar is converted into a verified availability list through two steps: 1. Gap Extraction: identifies all continuous free intervals from the daily schedule. 2. Duration Filter: filters these intervals, discarding any slot shorter than the target meeting duration. The resulting availability list is stored in the agent’s local memory. • Memory Structure: The memory mechanism consists of two parts. The local memory stores each agent’s verified availability list, while a structured team memory object tracks the public negotiation state, including candidate slots and rejected slots .• Observation: In each round, agents are prompted with their local availability memory instead of the raw busy schedule. They are instructed to propose or accept only slots that fit within their verified availability list. The current team memory is also provided as public context to avoid repeatedly proposing rejected slots. • Stateful Update: At the end of each round, the public conversation log is parsed to update the team memory . Newly proposed slots are added to candidate slots , while slots rejected by any agent are moved to rejected slots .E.2.3 PROTOCOL 

Protocol makes the scheduling negotiation more structured by replacing open-ended discussion with a phase-based procedure and typed outputs. This reduces ambiguity in candidate collection and voting, and makes the final consensus easier to verify. • Structured Phases: The negotiation is controlled by a finite-state protocol with three phases: 

– Phase 0: Broad Scan. Agents report coarse availability windows, such as Day 1 Morning .

– Phase 1: Propose. Agents propose concrete meeting slots (e.g., “10:00-10:20”) within the selected broad windows. 

– Phase 2: Vote. Agents vote (0/1) on consolidated candidates. • JSON-Only communication: Agents output a phase-specific JSON object rather than free-form natural language. For example, proposals use {"p": [[day, start, end]] },while votes use {"v": [1, 0, ...] }.E.2.4 PROMPT TEMPLATES

E.3 GAME 

The Game task is a cooperative grid-world resource collection task under partial observability. Agents collect resources and deposit them on warehouse tiles within a fixed number of rounds. The Base workflow defines the default environment interaction loop. Memory, Routing, and Protocol are then added as modular extensions to this workflow. E.3.1 BASE WORKFLOW 

The Base workflow defines the default execution loop for the Game task, where agents act solely based on their local observations. At each round, an agent receives a local view centered on itself, together with its global grid position, inventory state, current-tile information, visible terrain, visible resources, visible warehouse tiles, visible teammates, and task-specific fields such as the remaining capacity of the current tile. Coordinates follow the implementation convention: x increases from left to right, and y increases from bottom to top. Each agent generates one environment action per round. The action space includes movement actions 

UP , DOWN , LEFT , RIGHT , and STAY , as well as interaction actions PICK UP and PUT DOWN . For resource interactions, the parser also reads optional QTY and TYPE fields. An agent can carry only one resource type at a time. The PICK UP action is valid only on the agent’s current tile and is constrained by the available resource quantity on that tile and the agent’s remaining carrying capacity. The PUT DOWN 

action is also executed on the current tile and is constrained by the carried quantity, the remaining tile capacity, and, for warehouse tiles, the remaining per-type warehouse capacity. The environment resolves all agents’ actions synchronously. It first computes movement intentions, blocks moves into impassable terrain, resolves contested target cells and collisions, and then applies pickup/deposit interactions at the agents’ resulting positions. Deposited resources contribute to the score only when placed on compatible warehouse tiles, and each resource type is capped by its target quota. After all interactions are resolved, the environment updates the score, feedback, and round state. In the Base workflow, communication is inactive: messages enabled is set to false, no message phase is executed, and agents do not condition their actions on inter-agent messages. E.3.2 MEMORY 

Memory enables agents to accumulate discovered world knowledge across rounds, helping them reuse previously observed resources, warehouse tiles, and teammate information under partial observability. • Memory structure Each memory-enabled agent maintains a bounded known map as its per-sistent world state. Each entry records the location, terrain type, resource counts, warehouse capacity, per-type warehouse capacity, timestamp, information source, and staleness meta-data. In addition, the agent keeps compact buffers for recent responses, sent and received messages, and a team-state record when structured communication is available. • Memory update After each local observation, visible resources and warehouse-capacity information are merged into known map . Directly observed facts have priority over older message-derived facts. When communication is enabled, delivered messages are also parsed into structured facts about resources, warehouse tiles, goals, requests, and teammate states. The implementation combines deterministic parsing for compact message patterns with a strict JSON extractor for free-form resource or warehouse facts. • Observation In each action step, the prompt is augmented with memory-derived hints, including visible resource and warehouse summaries, relevant known map entries outside the current view, recent communication facts, and protocol team-state summaries when available. These hints help agents reuse previously discovered information under partial observability. 44 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • Bounded context. To keep the prompt compact, memory entries are bounded and pruned by recency and relevance. Only a small number of nearby or task-relevant resource and warehouse hints are injected into the prompt. Memory is used only as advisory context for action selection; the simulator still deterministically checks all movement, pickup, and deposit constraints. E.3.3 ROUTING 

Routing enables point-to-point communication among agents, allowing local discoveries to be selectively shared under partial observability. It only controls the communication decision, i.e., whether to send a message and which teammate should receive it, while the environment action is still generated and executed by the Base workflow. • Communication In each communication round, an agent can send at most one message to one teammate. Candidate recipients are teammates currently within communication range, excluding the sender itself. A queued message is addressed to a single recipient and becomes visible to that recipient in the next round if the recipient remains within communication range at delivery time. • Random routing In the Random routing variant, the receiver is sampled from the candidate recipient set using the configured deterministic random source. The sender does not decide whether to send or whom to send to; the LLM only generates the message content for the selected recipient. • Gate-LLM routing In the Gate-LLM routing variant, the routing decision is decomposed into two steps. The agent first decides whether to send a message through a SEND prompt. If sending is enabled, a second TO prompt selects one recipient from the candidate set. The routing step records the selected recipient and compact routing evidence for subsequent message generation, but it does not directly determine the final message body. E.3.4 PROTOCOL 

Protocol standardizes the content of inter-agent messages so that communication becomes more compact, grounded, and easier to parse. It constrains how a message is formatted after Routing has determined whether and to whom the agent sends a message. • Scope Routing is responsible for the communication decision, including whether to send a message and which teammate receives it. Protocol modifies message construction and validation, not the environment’s action space or execution rules. Changes in received messages may nevertheless affect subsequent action choices. • Message format When Protocol is enabled, this free-form payload is replaced by a structured message consisting of three parts: 

– STATE : a deterministic line containing the sender’s identity, position, carrying state, current goal, and action intent; 

– OBS : a grounded observation line selected from available evidence, such as resource information, warehouse-tile capacity, carry state, request, or status; 

– MSG : one short natural-language sentence generated by the LLM for a brief plan, request, or comment. • Grounding constraints Runtime guards are used to prevent unsupported or contradictory message content. In particular, the implementation rejects unsupported coordinates, invented quantities, unsupported capacity claims, and inconsistent warehouse-capacity statements. E.3.5 PROMPT TEMPLATES
