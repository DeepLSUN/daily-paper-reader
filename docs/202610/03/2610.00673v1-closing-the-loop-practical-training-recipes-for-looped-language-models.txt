# ABSTRACT 

Looped language models increase effective depth by repeatedly applying a shared block of layers, but existing large-scale recipes require multi-stage training over trillions of tokens, while the benefits of recurrence remain difficult to separate from differences in data and training. In this work, we establish practical train-ing recipes for looped language models, with three main results. (1) We de-velop a compute-efficient from-scratch pipeline that reduces the training budget from 7.7T tokens in Ouro to 310B tokens while retaining strong reasoning perfor-mance. Pretraining followed by high-quality mid-training, together with learning-rate warmup and stronger exit-gate regularization, enables stable recurrent train-ing without prior multi-stage schedules. (2) Under controlled comparisons, our 1.4B LoopLM outperforms a parameter-matched dense model trained on the same data and token budget on all 12 evaluated benchmarks, including +14 points on GSM8K, +10 on MATH, and +22 on DROP. At matched inference compute, it approaches a 3.9B dense model on mathematical reasoning and reading compre-hension while using only 36% as many parameters. (3) We introduce a minimal recipe for converting pretrained dense models into looped ones: a single learned input-mixing scalar and a smoothed exit loss, with no step-specific parameters. Applied to Qwen3-1.7B-Base, Looped Qwen improves over an identically contin-ued dense baseline on every evaluated benchmark across two data regimes, with statistically clear gains on GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-Pro on the curated mix-ture. Together, these results make looped language models substantially cheaper to train from scratch and practical to introduce into existing pretrained checkpoints, while isolating the gains due to recurrence itself. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Transformers spend the same computation on every token. Looped language models apply a shared transformer block repeatedly, which raises effective depth without adding parameters, and with adaptive exits they can spend extra computation only where it is needed (Dehghani et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2025). Ouro showed that looped models can be pretrained at scale and compete with much larger dense models (Zhu et al., 2025). Its recipe runs through five stages and 7.7T tokens, it starts from scratch while pretrained dense checkpoints go unused, and its gains are measured against dense models trained on other data, so the share that comes from recurrence is unknown. We address all three points: a simpler and much cheaper from-scratch recipe, a conversion of dense checkpoints into recurrent models with minimal architectural changes, and a comparison against dense counterparts trained on the same data with the same training stages, matched in parameters or in inference compute. Our recipe has two stages where Ouro has five, one pretraining run and one high-quality mid-training stage, and trains on 310B tokens against Ouro’s 7.7T. # 2 RELATED WORK 

Looped and recurrent language models. Universal Transformers introduced the construction behind most looped models, a shared block applied repeatedly with adaptive halting (Dehghani et al., 2019; Graves, 2016); fixed-point formulations replace the halting with a convergence test on the hidden state (Movahedi et al., 2026). Looped Transformers use the shared block to extend depth 2Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 and length generalization (Saunshi et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025), recurrent-depth models gain from more test-time iterations (Geiping et al., 2025), and Mixture-of-Recursions gives each token its own depth (Bae et al., 2025b). At scale, Ouro pretrains looped models that match much larger dense models (Zhu et al., 2025), HRM-Text applies hierarchical recurrence to open-domain text (Wang et al., 2026), and Loop the Loopies loops a mixture-of-experts model (Gao et al., 2026). 

Training stability. Each of these works hits an instability of its own. Ouro saw loss spikes at eight recurrent steps and cut the depth to four and halved the KL coefficient; the first large run of Geiping et al. (2025) stalled with hidden states that became the same for every token; HRM-Text adds a normalization scheme and warms up its backward horizon; Mixture-of-Recursions needs balancing losses to keep its router alive. None gives a from-scratch recipe without a multi-stage schedule or names the ingredient that keeps the exit gate alive once that schedule is gone (Section 3). 

Comparing looped and dense models. Comparisons against dense models trained on the same data exist: Saunshi et al. (2025) and Mixture-of-Recursions train parameter-matched and compute-matched baselines, HRM-Text matches training FLOPs, Loop the Loopies matches pretraining cost, and Ouro’s own small-scale study finds a deeper dense model ahead of the looped one at matched forward FLOPs. Ouro and the recurrent-depth model report their headline numbers against pub-lished dense models trained on other data, so the share of the gain that comes from recurrence stays open. We measure the model we report against dense twins in both regimes, and do the same for Looped Qwen, our converted model, where prior work matched parameters only. 

Converting pretrained models. Relaxed Recursive Transformers tie the layers of a pretrained model into looping blocks with one LoRA adapter per iteration and distill from the original (Bae et al., 2025a); Think-at-Hard starts from Qwen3-Base and adds depth-specific adapters and a router for hard tokens (Fu et al., 2025). Both rely on step-specific adapters and compare against parameter-matched baselines only. We show that looping the pretrained decoder as it is degrades the represen-tation, that one learned scalar stops this degradation and that adapters are optional. 

Reinforcement learning. GRPO and DAPO (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) assume a fixed computation path; RLTT distributes credit across recurrent iterations (Williams & Tureci, 2026). Ouro reports no gain from RL on its final model. A separate study asks when RL adds to a looped model (Appendix C). 

# 3 A SIMPLER FROM -SCRATCH RECIPE 

3.1 OURO ’S STAGED TRAINING 

Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) trains LoopLMs through five stages: pretraining at eight recurrent steps, con-tinued pretraining at four, high-quality annealing, context extension, and supervised mid-training. Its exit gate is optimized jointly with the language model using an entropy-regularized ELBO objective with KL coefficient β.We simplify this recipe by collapsing the pretraining stages into a single run, reducing the total token budget, and training the dense baselines of Section 5.1 through the same stages on the same data. 3.2 A SINGLE PRETRAINING STAGE 

Ouro’s pretraining uses three regimes before fine-tuning: pretraining at T =8 , continued pretraining at T =4 , and annealing on a higher-quality mixture (Zhu et al., 2025). We replace all three with one run from random initialization on a fixed mixture dominated by filtered web text, with mathematics and instruction-following data (Table 12). As in Loop the Loopies (Gao et al., 2026), high-quality data is present from the start. We keep only one mid-training stage afterwards (Section 3.4). 3.3 DEPTH AND STABILITY 

Our single run departs from Ouro’s first-stage configuration in depth and in schedule. 3Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 Ouro explores T =8 in its first stage and drops to four afterwards, citing loss spikes at the larger depth (Zhu et al., 2025). Our own T =8 run under that configuration points the same way: the per-loop loss stops improving after the third or fourth iteration, and the gate spreads its mass evenly over loops four to eight (Figure 2). The extra iterations cost compute without measurable gain, so depth is fixed at T =4 from the start.

(b) Exit probability per loop in the same run; the gate spreads its mass evenly over loops four to eight. (c) The same for the main T =4 run. Values are means over the last 5k steps. Ouro also lowers β after its first stage and trains with a constant learning rate from then on. Our run has no such transition, so we ask what keeps the exit gate alive once the schedule is gone. The failure is a gate collapse: early in training the gate learns to exit after the first loop, later iterations stop receiving gradient, and the model trains on as a plain transformer. Figure 3 compares β=0 .05 ,the value Ouro uses after its first stage, with our β=0 .15 , each trained without warmup and with our warmup-plus-cosine schedule. Without warmup the smaller β collapses for good and the larger one comes close before it recovers. With the warmup schedule the larger β never comes near the collapsed regime, while the smaller one stays near p0 = 0 .9 for its first thousand steps before recov-ering. Thus, warmup prevents early collapse at our β, while larger β enables recovery if collapse occurs: without warmup every β ≥ 0.10 we tried recovers, and only β=0 .05 stays collapsed (Ap-pendix B). The main run therefore uses β=0 .15 with warmup and cosine decay (Table 1). Notably, the language-modelling loss does not reveal the collapse while it occurs, so the exit distribution must be monitored directly.

Despite the mathematics and instruction-following data in pretraining, the resulting checkpoint reaches only 35% accuracy on 5-shot GSM8K (Appendix C). We therefore add a high-quality mid-training stage using instruction–response pairs with loss on response tokens only. The mixture emphasizes mathematics and reasoning while retaining a knowledge-preserving component. Mid-training continues for 100B tokens on the HRM-Text mixture (Wang et al., 2026), whose largest components are FLAN, SYNTH, and OpenMathInstruct-2 (Table 13). Recurrent depth, β, weight decay, optimizer, and architecture remain unchanged from pretraining. The same stage is applied to both dense baselines with identical data, schedule, and token budget. 3.5 OUR IMPROVED SETUP 

The final recipe keeps Ouro’s shared recurrent architecture and trains it in two stages instead of five: a single 210B-token pretraining run from random initialization followed by 100B tokens of high-quality mid-training. The resulting 310B-token budget is 25 × smaller than the 7.7T tokens used for Ouro-1.4B and needs about 3% of the training compute of Ouro’s schedule on the same architecture (Appendix I). Training uses T =4 from the start, a learning-rate warmup to prevent early exit-gate collapse, and β=0 .15 to enable recovery if collapse occurs (Section 3.3). The mid-training stage contributes most of the reasoning improvement: relative to the step-100,000 pretraining checkpoint, it more than doubles GSM8K accuracy and improves MMLU by about nine points. 3.6 REINFORCEMENT LEARNING AS A SEPARATE STUDY 

None of the models compared in Section 5 uses reinforcement learning; we study it separately, since Ouro reported no gain from RL with verifiable rewards on its final checkpoint (Zhu et al., 2025). We use RLTT (Williams & Tureci, 2026), a GRPO variant for looped models that distributes policy gradients across recurrent iterations while keeping rollout and training execution paths consistent. With group-based methods a prompt contributes gradient only when its rollouts contain both suc-cesses and failures. A mid-trained checkpoint of an earlier run of this recipe already solved about three quarters of its MATH rollouts, and RLTT left MATH unchanged; the step-100,000 pretraining checkpoint of the main run solved about a third of its GSM8K rollouts, and the same method added twelve points. What separates the two runs is the solve rate at rollout time. # 4 LOOPING PRETRAINED LLM S

Only a few pretrained looped checkpoints are publicly available, making dense-to-loop conversion an attractive alternative to training from scratch. We find that naive recurrence degrades later steps, while a single input-preserving scalar together with a smoothed exit loss is sufficient to convert Qwen3-1.7B-Base, at under 1% of its pretraining compute (Appendix I). Appendix G reports the corresponding training dynamics. 4.1 CONVERSION RECIPE 

We start from Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Yang et al., 2025) and apply the full decoder recurrently for T =3 

steps. Without input re-injection, later steps are consistently worse than the first: after the initial phase, the LM losses of steps 2 and 3 remain about 0.01 and 0.02 nats higher, respectively (Fig-ure 9a). A learned per-step projection of the original embedding and recurrent state also fails to produce refinement: the gate abandons the middle step and the final step does not improve over the first (Figure 9c). We instead preserve direct access to the original embedding with a single learned scalar. Let e denote the token embedding and ht−1 the output of the previous recurrent step. For t > 0,

zt = we + (1 − w)ht−1, w = σ(a), (1) where a is the only additional parameter. With the same data, batch, and seed as the naive run, steps 2 and 3 finish about 0.02 nats below step 1 (Figure 9b). We initialize w = 0 .99 ; it increases slowly throughout training in all runs (Figure 9e; Appendix F). 4.2 TRAINING STABILITY

We use a curated continued-training mixture covering mathematics, code, STEM, science, and instruction following, with final-answer supervision and no explicit chain-of-thought traces (Ap-pendix F). The conversion result is consistent across both data regimes. Without step-specific adapters, Looped Qwen improves over Qwen3-1.7B-Base continued on exactly the same data for the same number of steps on every benchmark in both runs. Most gains are modest, but the curated instruction mixture produces clear reasoning improvements: +4.3 points on GSM8K, +3.8 on MMLU-Pro, and +2.2 on MATH, each exceeding two standard errors of the difference. On the web-scale mixture, gains remain within about two points, with only MMLU and HellaSwag exceeding that threshold. The larger gains on the curated mixture suggest that the benefit of recurrence depends strongly on the continued-training data. Importantly, the effect does not require step-specific capacity: the shared and LoRA-32 variants differ by at most two points, with neither consistently ahead. Looped Qwen still trails Qwen3-4B-Base on every benchmark. The conversion therefore does not replace the knowledge and capacity obtained from substantially larger pretraining; instead, it pro-vides a lightweight way to improve an existing 1.7B checkpoint without repeating pretraining or adding per-step parameters. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

This work gives two practical ways to build a looped language model: a short recipe for training one from scratch, and a conversion of a pretrained dense model with minimal architectural changes. For from-scratch training, a two-stage recipe trains a looped model at a fraction of the token budget of earlier multi-stage pipelines, a learning-rate warmup removes the early collapse of the exit gate, and a larger KL coefficient lets the gate recover if it does collapse. The resulting LoopLM improves over a parameter-matched dense model on every evaluated benchmark and, at equal inference com-pute, comes within about a point of a dense model with three times its parameters on math and reading comprehension; the larger model keeps its lead where parameter count stores facts. For pretrained models, applying a dense decoder repeatedly does not produce refinement. One learned scalar that mixes the token embedding into each recurrent step, together with a smoothed exit loss, is enough to turn Qwen3-1.7B-Base into Looped Qwen, which improves on the source checkpoint given the same continued training, with no step-specific parameters. At the inference cost of Qwen3-4B-Base it still trails that model, as a checkpoint more than twice its size should. # A LAYER -NORMALIZATION ABLATION 

Weight sharing makes normalization unusually important in recurrent models: the same decoder block is traversed up to T times, and its parameters receive gradients accumulated across recur-rent iterations. Prior work has likewise identified normalization as an important stability choice in deeply recurrent Transformers: HRM-Text uses a parameter-free pre-norm variant, MagicNorm (Wang et al., 2026), while recurrent-depth pretraining has shown strong sensitivity to normalization placement (Geiping et al., 2025). We ablate two choices separately: (i) normalization inside the recurrent decoder block ( decoder norm ), and (ii) normalization of the hidden state before the shared LM head ( final norm ). Unless stated otherwise, all runs use the architecture and optimizer of our main recipe, T =4 , a 500K-token batch, and a matched budget of 19K steps. NORMALIZATION SCHEMES 

For the decoder block, we compare five variants. Ouro-norm is the configuration used by Zhu et al. (2025) and by our main recipe (Section 3): it normalizes the input and the output of each sub-layer and adds the normalized output to the residual stream. Pre-norm applies normalization before each attention or MLP sub-layer, followed by the residual addition. Post-norm normalizes after the residual addition. Sandwich-norm also normalizes the input of each sub-layer, but applies the second normalization after the residual addition, so the residual stream itself is normalized, following Geiping et al. (2025). Finally, Magic-norm is pre-norm with the learnable normalization scale removed, following Wang et al. (2026). For the final hidden state, we test learnable RMSNorm ( RMS ), parameter-free RMSNorm ( FRMS ), and no normalization ( identity ). # B EARLY PRETRAINING COLLAPSE TO A DENSE REGIME 

Section 3.3 shows that learning-rate warmup removes the early gate collapse. Here we examine the same failure mode more closely in the regime where it can actually occur: training without warmup. Rather than repeating the warmup × β comparison from Figure 3, we sweep β to characterize when an early collapse becomes permanent and when the model recovers. All runs use the same 2M-token-batch configuration as the main recipe (Table 1). We train without warmup at β ∈ { 0.05 , 0.10 , 0.50 } using a constant learning rate, and include one no-warmup cosine-schedule run at β=0 .15 , whose cosine spans a long budget, so its learning rate stays near the peak over the steps shown. We track the probability of exiting after the first recurrent step, p0, which directly measures how close the model is to single-step dense training. COLLAPSE IS USUALLY REVERSIBLE 

The sweep shows that entering the dense regime does not by itself imply a training failure. Three of the four runs enter the collapsed regime early in training: at β ∈ { 0.05 , 0.10 , 0.15 } the first-step exit probability reaches 0.99 to 1.00 within the first few hundred steps. At β=0 .10 and β=0 .15 the gate then returns to a recurrent regime, with late p0 of 0.11 and 0.14; both had recovered by about step 1K. At β=0 .05 it stays at p0=1 .00 for all 2,267 steps of the run. At β=0 .50 the gate never enters the collapsed regime: after the first 50 steps p0 peaks at 0.66. Without warmup, a collapse is therefore the usual start of training at moderate β, and β decides whether the gate recovers from it. This failure mode is different from the normalization failure analyzed in Appendix A. Here, the shared decoder block continues to receive gradients and train; what disappears is the contribution of later recurrent steps because the exit gate places nearly all probability on the first step. Under post-norm and sandwich-norm, by contrast, the gate is not the primary problem. Gradients vanish through almost the entire decoder block, leaving essentially only its final layer trainable. That failure is persistent and is not repaired by changing β or extending warmup. The two cases therefore correspond to different forms of effective model collapse: gate collapse reduces a recurrent model to single-step computation, temporarily or permanently, whereas normal-ization failure reduces a deep recurrent block to effectively single-layer optimization.

We also tried re-injecting the original token embeddings at every recurrent step: concatenate the initial embedding with the current state, then project back to hidden size before the next loop, fol-lowing the input-injection construction of Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) and later recurrent-depth models (Geiping et al., 2025). We crossed two choices for the injection’s initial state 

s0 (a learned zero vector vs. a truncated-normal draw) with two projection schemes (one shared pro-jection vs. one per step), and compared all four against an otherwise identical injection-free run. The comparison is on training LM loss; none of these runs was evaluated downstream. Table 14 reports the loss at a matched budget of 40K steps (2M-token batch, β=0 .15 , constant learn-ing rate, no warmup). The four injection variants sit in a band of width 0.01 around 1.90 –1.91 , which is 0.12 –0.13 above the injection-free baseline ( 1.78 ). A per-step projection with a truncated-normal 

s0 is the lowest of the four, by 0.005 , which we do not treat as a real difference. Concatenation injection therefore did not help in this recipe, and we keep the injection-free formulation.

# J THINK -AT -H ARD 

We also reproduced Think-at-Hard (TaH) (Fu et al., 2025) on our training mixture. TaH uses a static oracle policy to identify tokens that a frozen reference model predicts incorrectly on its first pass. The backbone and deeper-step LoRA adapters are then trained to refine these hard tokens, after which a lightweight decider is trained to imitate the oracle policy at inference. In our reproduction, the repeated-step LM loss never fell below the first-step loss, and accuracy degraded substantially on several downstream benchmarks. Thus, we could not reproduce the gains reported in the original work under our data and training setup. Despite this negative result, correctness-directed routing is an appealing alternative to the cumulative exit probabilities used by LoopLM. A deeper step receives supervision specifically where the shallower step fails, rather than learning a stopping probability only through its contribution to the expected LM loss. This directly connects additional computation to errors that still need to be corrected and may provide a cleaner objective for token-level adaptive depth. The binary top-1 oracle used for two-step TaH does not directly specify how many iterations a hard token needs. An exact correctness-based extension to three steps would require labels indicating whether each token is first solved at step 0, 1, or 2. If these labels are precomputed, step 1 must first be trained before its errors can be measured, followed by another labeling pass and another training run for the three-step model. Repeating this procedure up to depth T would require a sequence of shallower models and dataset relabeling stages. The current TaH paper avoids this cost for TaH-3 by assigning depths through quantiles of a frozen reference model’s loss. This is efficient, but the resulting depth is a proxy for difficulty rather than the first recurrent step that actually predicts the token correctly. We propose generating the depth mask online from the looped model itself. During training, we execute the maximum active number of recurrent steps and compare the top-1 prediction at every step with the target token. For token position j, we select 

dj = min {t : arg max  

> v

qt(v | x≤j ) = xj+1 }, (9) where qt is the token distribution at recurrent step t, and apply its LM gradient only at depth dj .If no step predicts the target correctly, we assign the token to the final active step. The discrete 27 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 mask is treated as a stop-gradient routing decision. Unlike static oracle labels, it always reflects the current model and requires neither a separate reference model nor an offline relabeling pass. Naively enabling all depths from the start can starve intermediate steps of supervision. In a newly initialized three-step model, step 1 will often remain incorrect, so many tokens are routed directly from step 0 to step 2; step 1 then receives too few updates to improve. We address this with a depth curriculum. For a three-step model, the first half of training activates only steps 0 and 1, assigning all tokens missed by both to step 1. Step 2 is introduced in the second half, after step 1 has learned a useful refinement. More generally, the maximum active depth is increased one step at a time during training. This curriculum is intended to combine TaH’s correctness-directed supervision with more than two recurrent steps, while avoiding the intermediate-step collapse that makes a fully dynamic oracle unstable. We leave a controlled evaluation of this training rule to future work. 28
