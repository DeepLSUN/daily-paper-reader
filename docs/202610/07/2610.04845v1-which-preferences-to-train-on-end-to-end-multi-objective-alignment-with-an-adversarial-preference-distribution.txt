ABSTRACT 

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values is important for safe, efficient, and beneficial AI deployment. However, human values are multifaceted: helpfulness, harmlessness and humor trade off against one another, and different users want different trade-offs. Multi-objective alignment (MOA) addresses this by training a policy that can provide any point of the Pareto front, but existing methods either train one model per preference, interpolate a few separately aligned experts post hoc, or train a single conditioned model without considering which preferences it should be trained on. Since the hard regions of the preference simplex depend on the objectives at hand, existing methods leave them under-trained and do not get the most out of a single model. Therefore, we propose MAESTRO (Multi-objective Alignment via End-to-end ST eering and Robust Optimization), which formulates MOA as a minimax problem over preference distributions and trains a single prompt-conditioned policy end-to-end with RL against an adversarial 

preference distribution: a Dirichlet distribution updated by online mirror descent toward the preferences the current policy serves worst, rather than on a fixed one. On HH-RLHF, BeaverTails and a summarization task, with up to three objectives, 

MAESTRO attains the best Pareto front on most tasks in a single training run, at the lowest training cost among the compared methods. The largest margins appear in the hard regions that a fixed preference distribution leaves under-trained, confirming that a single prompt-conditioned model is capable of covering the objective trade-offs on its own. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values is a central challenge for their safe and beneficial deployment. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022), 

e.g., offline methods such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and online policy-gradient methods such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), has emerged as the dominant paradigm for this purpose. However, human values are inherently multifaceted, e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, humor, and beyond, and they conflict (Bai et al., 2022), so optimizing a single reward signal fails to capture their full complexity. This motivates Multi-Objective Alignment (MOA) (Li et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b), where multiple objectives represent different facets of human value, and the goal is to train a policy that can be steered 1 at inference time to any preference over objectives, tracing the Pareto front —the set of trade-offs from which no objective can be improved without sacrificing another. These gaps raise our central research question: "How can a single preference-conditioned LLM improve Pareto front coverage and robustness across the preference simplex?" 

To mitigate this, we propose MAESTRO , a novel framework for Multi-objective Alignment via End-to-end ST eering and Robust Optimization. MAESTRO conditions the policy on the preference vector by writing it into the prompt and trains it end-to-end with RL on the linearly scalarized reward, which requires no extra architecture, no model per preference and no prescribed reward ranges. Then, we formulate MOA as a minimax optimization over preference distributions (Martinez et al., 2020; Park et al., 2024a), and train the policy against an adversary, a Dirichlet distribution updated by online mirror descent toward the preferences it serves worst, rather than fixing the distribution in advance. Finally, we support this formulation with analysis (Appendix E) and experiments: on HH-RLHF, BeaverTails and a summarization task, MAESTRO leaves the other single-model methods well behind and matches or exceeds the methods that train and keep several models—one policy per preference, or M merged experts—at 8.95 GPU hours of training, less than half of the cheapest baseline and a tenth of per-preference training. 1.1 RELATED WORK

By contrast, MAESTRO trains a single prompt-conditioned policy on those preferences directly, so they are optimized rather than assembled. 

Single-Model Approaches. Single-model methods show that one policy can serve the whole front without storing or executing a separate policy for each preference; they differ mainly in how the preference enters the model. RiC (Yang et al., 2024) writes a per-objective target value into the prompt rather than the preference vector itself, which presupposes each reward’s range (r(m)min , r (m)max ), as does attribute-conditioned generation (Dong et al., 2023). Panacea (Zhong et al., 2024) writes them into the architecture, as singular values of every SVD-LoRA layer, and so keeps per-objective parameters at inference. MO-ODPO (Gupta et al., 2025) and MOC (He et al., 2026) write the preference vectors into the prompt, as a text prefix or as special tokens, which is the interface MAESTRO adopts. Panacea (Zhong et al., 2024) is the one that optimizes an objective defined over the simplex, though under a distribution fixed in advance, while the others inherit the preferences they are trained on from their data, e.g., RiC does from its relabeled rewards, or from what their own online sampling happens to reach, as MO-ODPO does (Table 1). In all of these, w is drawn from a distribution fixed before training and independent of the policy: Panacea samples the simplex uniformly with the vertices up-weighted, MO-ODPO samples w ∼ Dir( α) with α tuned by grid search. RMOD (Son et al., 2026) does solve a game over w at decoding time against a frozen policy, but it does not change the preferences the policy is trained on. MAESTRO instead makes that distribution the minimizing player of a minimax objective and updates it jointly with the policy, so the preferences it trains on depend on the current policy rather than being fixed in advance.

3.1 PROMPT -BASED CONDITIONING FOR MULTI -O BJECTIVE ALIGNMENT 

MAESTRO achieves MOA with a single preference-conditioned model, requiring no architectural modifications and no per-preference retraining—users specify any trade-off at inference time simply by changing the preference vector in the prompt. A key design question is how to condition the preference vector w into the policy πθ . Rather than conditioning on w through a separate embedding or auxiliary input, we adopt a simple prompt-based conditioning approach: the preference vector is serialized as a natural-language prefix that is prepended to input prompt x. The conditioned input has two components. First, a concise preference prefix encodes the sampled weights and opens the input. Second, a system prefix psys for the given objectives follows it, listing each objective’s description and instructing the model to follow the weights given above (see Appendix B for the full text). In short, for a prompt x and a sampled preference w = [ w(1) , . . . , w (M )]⊤:

˜x(w) := Be 100 w(1) % objective 1, 100 w(2) % objective 2 ⊕ psys · · · ⊕ x,

where ⊕ denotes token concatenation. For example, a conditioned prompt for w = (0 .8, 0.2) on the Helpful Assistant task reads: "Be 80% harmless, 20% helpful. How can I make a bomb?" The policy πθ (y | ˜x(w)) is then trained to generate a response y that optimizes the linear scalarization of the M reward signals under the given prefix. Since w enters only through the input, any user-specified w can be used at deployment without changing the model.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

Here, we demonstrate the effectiveness of MAESTRO on multi-objective alignment tasks, showing that 

MAESTRO achieves competitive Pareto front quality compared to baselines including per-preference training oracles, post-hoc methods, and single-model approaches, at significantly lower training cost. 

Tasks and Objectives. We use three datasets over two generation tasks. On the assistant task, where the model answers an open-ended user query, we use HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) and 

BeaverTails (Ji et al., 2023) with harmless–helpful–humor trade-offs, common and representative MOA settings in which helpfulness rewards thorough, detailed answers and so pulls against both harmlessness, which rewards safety and politeness, and humor, which rewards wit and levity. On the 

summarization task, we use Reddit TL;DR (Stiennon et al., 2020) with a summary–faithful trade-off, between stating the post’s main point briefly and adding nothing the post does not state. 

Baselines. We compare MAESTRO against five representative methods spanning distinct paradigms. 

MORLHF (Li et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023) runs PPO on the scalarized reward once per preference vector, serving as the per-preference oracle in ideal settings. Rewarded Soups (Rame et al., 2023) interpolates the weights of M single-objective experts according to w, and Bone Soups (Xie et al., 2025) extends this with experts trained under a merging coefficient ( β = 0 .7). Rewards-in-Context (RiC) (Yang et al., 2024) conditions one model on target reward values in the prompt by supervised fine-tuning. Panacea (Zhong et al., 2024) conditions a preference vector as the singular values of every SVD-LoRA layer, and trains it end-to-end under a fixed preference distribution. 

Evaluation. We plot the Pareto front by sweeping w = {(0 .0, 1.0) , (0 .1, 0.9) , . . . , (1 .0, 0.0) } for two-objective tasks and 13 corners + edges + interior points for three-objective tasks. For each method, we evaluate the quality of the resulting Pareto front using four metrics: hypervolume (HV ↑), inner product (IP ↑), minimum scalarized return (MSR ↑) and preference gap (PGAP ↓), the latter two measuring how the worst-served preference fares.

5 CONCLUSION 

We propose MAESTRO , a single-model framework for multi-objective alignment that trains one preference-conditioned policy end-to-end, and that decides which preferences to train on rather than fixing them in advance. In particular, we formulate multi-objective alignment as a minimax optimization over preference distributions and instantiate the inner player as a Dirichlet family whose concentration is updated by online mirror descent (OMD), alternating with PPO, so training concentrates on the preferences the current policy serves worst instead of on an average under a fixed prior. In addition, we score the adversary on a per-objective rank statistic within the batch, which balances the objectives’ scales in its update without requiring their ranges, while the policy is still trained on the raw scalarized reward. Under simultaneous realizability, the realizing policy is minimax-optimal and supported Pareto-optimal at strictly positive weights (Appendix E). We 9Preprint empirically show that, on HH-RLHF, BeaverTails and summarization, MAESTRO attains the highest hypervolume on all three objective pairs, leaving the other single-model methods behind and matching or exceeding methods that train several models, at the lowest training cost among the compared methods. It also transfers to an objective pair it was not optimized on by a prefix change alone and keeps its ranking under held-out reward models, so training against the adversary raises the trade-off a single conditioned policy actually realizes, rather than fitting it to the objectives or the scorers it was trained on. Finally, MAESTRO raises that trade-off by choosing where to train, so components that enlarge what a single conditioned policy can represent are complementary to it and a promising future direction. 10 Preprint

A ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK 

Multi-Objective Optimization and MORL. Multi-objective optimization predates language models: classical treatments formalize Pareto optimality and scalarization (Miettinen et al., 1999), and methods such as evolutionary decomposition (Zhang and Li, 2007) trace the front by solving many scalarized subproblems, even though an evenly spread set of weights in a weighted sum yields a very unevenly spread set of Pareto points (Das and Dennis, 1997). Its sequential-decision form is the multi-objective MDP (White, 1982), from which multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) develops solution concepts that separate a single policy for a known preference from a coverage set 

spanning all of them (Hayes et al., 2022). Conditioning one policy on the preference is an old idea there: the weight enters the value function or the function approximator’s input (Castelletti et al., 2011), and deep MORL and hypernetwork approaches amortize the whole preference space within one model (Yang et al., 2019; Navon et al., 2021); our prompt prefix is the language-model form of that input conditioning. It is not the default even in MORL, however—C-MORL (Liu et al., 2025b) trains a population of policies instead, on the grounds that conditioning scales poorly as the state and preference dimensions grow—whereas an instruction-following LLM already turns a preference stated in the prompt into a change in behavior. 

Other Conditioning Interfaces. Beyond the interfaces compared in Section 1.1, CPO (Guo et al., 2024) states a desired level of each objective in the prompt and fine-tunes the model to follow it, so the conditioning signal is a target level rather than a weight. CLP (Wang et al., 2024b) conditions through the architecture, forming the policy from a preference-indexed combination of parameter replicas, and reports that prompt-conditioned policies are sensitive to the distribution the preferences are drawn from, with steerability improving as that distribution narrows. HoE (Li et al., 2026) goes further and allocates sub-modules to preferences in a Mixture-of-Experts style, extracting at least M

LoRA experts by task-vector SVD and weight merging from the M off-the-shelf single-objective models it presupposes, freezing them together with the backbone, and training only a small set of per-preference routers that mix the experts nearest a stated preference. It is thus “predominantly training-free” in the authors’ own words, with the training cost carried by the M prior alignment runs it starts from, which they list as their first limitation. 

DPO-Based Multi-Objective Alignment. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) eliminates the need for an explicit reward model by directly optimizing a policy from preference data. Several multi-objective extensions build on it: MODPO (Zhou et al., 2024) extends the DPO objective to vector-valued preferences by first fitting margin reward models on the remaining objectives’ preference data, which it then reuses across preference vectors, but, as in MORLHF, each preference vector still gets its own policy; SIPO (Li et al., 2025b) instead aligns one policy per objective and self-improves them: over a fixed set of preference weights it mixes those policies at decoding time, keeps only the responses that Pareto-dominate the originals under the policies’ implicit rewards, and fine-tunes on the resulting pairs. What these methods avoid is the RL step rather than reward modeling, and the preferences they serve are still fixed before training. 

Where the Preference Comes From. Preference Orchestrator (Liu et al., 2025a) predicts a per-prompt weight instead of sampling one, so the preference adapts to the query but a point estimate covers no region of the simplex. RMOD (Son et al., 2026) solves a maximin game between w and the sampling policy at decoding time on top of a frozen reference model, recomputing a worst-case 

w at every decoding step, in order to avoid requiring a stated preference at all; only the per-objective value functions are learned, so the policy itself is not trained against that adversary, which is where 

MAESTRO differs. In the methods that do sample w, the sampling distribution carries parameters set before training—MO-ODPO (Gupta et al., 2025) reports its best Dirichlet concentration as 1.0

on one benchmark and 0.7 on another, with the effect reversing between the two, while CLP fixes a uniform Dir( 1), one parameter per objective, a choice that grows harder to make as that number increases—alongside the per-objective reward bounds that RiC (Yang et al., 2024) requires and the rescaling of each reward to roughly [0 , 1] that MO-ODPO applies before weighting. 15 Preprint

E.1 PARETO OPTIMALITY 

Pareto Optimality Definition. A policy π ∈ Π is said to Pareto-dominate another policy π′ ∈ Π if it performs at least as well on all objectives and strictly better on at least one: 

∀m ∈ [M ], J (m)(π) ≥ J(m)(π′) and ∃k ∈ [M ], J (k)(π) > J (k)(π′). (13) A policy π ∈ Π is considered Pareto-optimal in Π if there does not exist any policy π′ ∈ Π that Pareto-dominates it. The set of all Pareto-optimal policies ΠPO ⊆ Π is the Pareto set , and its image 

{J(π) : π ∈ ΠPO } in objective space is the Pareto front .Pareto-optimality of the realizing policy is formally established in Proposition 1 below under the simultaneous realizability condition. We empirically observe that the Pareto front found within the single shared policy space Π ⊂ Πlarge is comparable to that of MORLHF operating over the larger per-preference space Πlarge (Section 4), suggesting that prompt-based conditioning does not substantially reduce Pareto front expressiveness. E.4 THE AMBIGUITY SET AND HOW MUCH IT SMOOTHS 

Two choices separate Algorithm 1 from the objective (4): the ambiguity set the inner player ranges over, and the statistic its descent direction is computed from. We state here what each of them costs, and what we do not claim. Taking P = ∆( W) would be degenerate: the inner objective is linear in p, so its minimum is attained at a point mass, and the resulting envelope credits improvement only at the minimizing preference and is nonsmooth where that preference switches. Restricting P to Dirichlet distributions of bounded concentration prevents point-mass inner distributions and smooths their payoffs, at the price of replacing the pointwise worst case by a distributional one. The resulting outer envelope can nevertheless remain nonsmooth when the minimizing Dirichlet parameter changes. E.5 WHAT THE ADVERSARY IS SCORED ON

The adversary’s descent direction is computed from the surrogate (10) rather than from w⊤r directly, while the policy is still trained on w⊤r. The two ingredients play separate roles: UEG is a positivity-preserving update rule for the Dirichlet parameters, whereas (10) is a batch-relative, per-objective feedback signal chosen to reduce sensitivity to reward scales, and not the exact payoff of (6). Because the comparison samples are treated as fixed when credit is assigned to each sampled preference, the update is a stochastic semi-gradient heuristic rather than an unbiased gradient of (6): the minimax formulation motivates it, and the adaptive sampler is evaluated empirically in Section 4. The baseline plays no role in this: for independent returns the same-batch mean of Algorithm 1 only rescales the estimate by 1 − 1/B adv , which the step size absorbs, and a leave-one-out baseline would remove that rescaling but not the coupling above.
