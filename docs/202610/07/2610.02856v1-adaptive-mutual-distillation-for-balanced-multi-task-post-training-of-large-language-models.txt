# ABSTRACT 

Multi-task post-training of large language models (LLMs) aims to improve per-formance across tasks with unequal amounts of training data. Existing methods focus primarily on balancing task contributions during single-model training. Dif-ferent task-balancing strategies can produce models with complementary strengths, creating opportunities for mutual distillation. However, the usefulness of cross-model supervision can vary across tasks, transfer directions, and stages of training. We propose Adaptive Mutual Distillation (AMD) , a collaborative post-training framework that jointly trains two models with different task-balancing strategies. AMD evaluates candidate adjustments to distillation weights through short train-ing probes shared across tasks, then uses task-wise validation scores to select an adjustment for each task and transfer direction. Across six benchmarks and three LLM backbones, both AMD models achieve higher average benchmark scores than supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baselines trained with the same sampling strategies. They also outperform the task-balancing methods evaluated in our experiments. Merging the two trained models can further improve their average benchmark score while yielding a single model for inference. The merged models outperform multi-task SFT by an average of 2.91 points across the three backbones. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on multi-task instruction mixtures is widely used to adapt large language models (LLMs) to a range of tasks (Sanh et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). These mixtures often contain unequal amounts of data across tasks. Under proportional sampling, tasks with more data are sampled more often. Increasing the emphasis on underrepresented tasks can improve their performance, but may reduce performance on other tasks. Improving overall multi-task performance requires managing these trade-offs. Existing methods primarily manage these trade-offs by adjusting task contributions within a single model. Data-mixture methods change how training examples are sampled (Xie et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2026), while optimization-based methods reweight task losses or modify gradients (Chen et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Multi-task LLM fine-tuning methods also regulate task contributions through convergence feedback, task-specific masks, and task-balanced objectives (Gong et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Different strategies place different emphases on tasks and can produce models with complementary strengths. These differences create an opportunity for models to improve through collaboration. Mutual distillation enables jointly trained models to exchange supervision (Zhang et al., 2018; Anil et al., 2018). The value of this supervision depends on the task and transfer direction. A model may provide useful guidance on one task while benefiting from its partner’s guidance on another. These relationships can also change as both models learn. We propose Adaptive Mutual Distillation (AMD) , a collaborative post-training framework that jointly trains two models with different task-balancing strategies. In our experiments, one model samples tasks in proportion to their frequencies in the training data. The other uses temperature-smoothed sampling to increase the relative sampling probabilities of tasks with less data. Both models first undergo independent SFT warmup. They then continue training under their respective sampling distributions while exchanging supervision through mutual distillation. Task-wise validation feedback guides the distillation weights for each task and transfer direction. Figure 1 summarizes the two-stage framework and optional post-training model merging. At each weight update, AMD runs three short training probes from the current model pair, testing whether to decrease, maintain, or increase all distillation weights. It then uses the resulting validation scores to select an adjustment for each task and transfer direction. We evaluate AMD on three LLM backbones and six benchmarks. On each backbone, both AMD models achieve higher average benchmark scores than SFT baselines trained with the same sampling strategies. They also outperform the task-balancing methods evaluated in our experiments. Merg-ing the two models can further improve performance while yielding a single model for inference. The merged models achieve the highest average benchmark scores among the evaluated methods, exceeding multi-task SFT by 2.70–3.24 points. Our contributions are as follows: • We propose AMD, a collaborative framework for multi-task LLM post-training with im-balanced task data. AMD jointly trains models with different task-balancing strategies and enables them to exchange task-wise supervision. • We develop a validation-guided mechanism that evaluates candidate distillation-weight adjustments through shared training probes and selects updates separately for each task and transfer direction. It adapts supervision throughout training while keeping the number of training probes per update fixed as the number of tasks grows. • Experiments on three LLM backbones and six benchmarks show that both AMD models outperform their sampling-matched SFT baselines in macro-average performance. Con-trolled comparisons support adapting distillation weights over training and separately for each task and transfer direction. Model merging provides additional gains while yielding a single model for inference. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Multi-task learning. Task imbalance and interference are common challenges in multi-task learning. Existing approaches adjust task losses, gradients, or the training data mixture to regulate task 2Figure 2: Adaptive distillation-weight update. Three probes globally decrease, retain, or increase the weights. Validation scores determine the weight adjustment for each task and receiving model. contributions. GradNorm balances task training rates through gradient normalization (Chen et al., 2018), PCGrad modifies conflicting gradients (Yu et al., 2020), and FAMO adaptively balances task losses during optimization (Liu et al., 2023). Optimization-based approaches also address task balance in multi-task LLM fine-tuning. CoBa uses convergence signals to adjust task weights (Gong et al., 2024), MFTCoder uses balanced multi-task losses (Liu et al., 2024), and MaZO combines task-specific masks with zeroth-order optimization (Zhang et al., 2025). At the data level, DoReMi (Xie et al., 2023), Mixture-of-Skills (Wu et al., 2024), and DynamixSFT (Shin et al., 2026) control the relative contributions of different data sources. HBO balances data allocation across and within datasets (Wang et al., 2026). AMD brings models with different task-balancing strategies into a collaborative training framework. Each model retains its own strategy while learning from its partner through mutual distillation. 

Mutual distillation. Mutual distillation enables concurrently trained models to exchange super-vision (Zhang et al., 2018; Anil et al., 2018). OKDDip learns aggregation weights to combine supervision from multiple peers (Chen et al., 2020). Online distillation has also been studied in multi-task learning, where jointly trained single-task networks guide a multi-task network through adaptive feature distillation and task weighting (Jacob et al., 2023). Other approaches adapt how teacher supervision is used. MMKD uses meta-learning to adjust the contributions of multiple pretrained teachers based on feedback from student updates (Zhang et al., 2023). DTO-KD dynami-cally balances task and distillation losses using their gradients (Hayder et al., 2026). AMD jointly trains two multi-task models with different task-balancing strategies. It uses task-wise validation scores from shared training probes to adjust distillation weights separately for each task and transfer direction as collaborative training progresses. 

# 3 METHODOLOGY 

AMD uses two training stages: independent SFT warmup and collaborative training with adaptive mutual distillation. Validation feedback guides the distillation weights for each task and transfer direction. Algorithm 1 summarizes the adaptive weight updates used in the second stage. After training, the two models can optionally be merged into a single model for inference (Section 5.4). 3.1 FRAMEWORK 

We consider a training mixture in which each example has a task label. Both models share the same data, architecture, and initialization. We index the tasks participating in mutual distillation by 

k ∈ { 1, . . . , K }. General instruction-following data are included in the training mixture for SFT, but do not participate in mutual distillation.

Stage 2: SFT with Adaptive Mutual Distillation. After warmup, each model continues training on batches drawn from its own sampling distribution. For tasks participating in mutual distillation, its partner provides token-level predictions on the same examples as distillation targets. Both models are updated through their own training objectives, so the supervision they provide evolves during training and reflects their current token-level predictions. Let u ← v denote distillation from model v to model u. For task k, the receiving model uses the following training objective: 

L =  1 − αu←vk

LSFT + αu←vk LKD .

Here, LSFT is the supervised fine-tuning loss. LKD is the KL divergence from the supervising model’s token distribution to the receiving model’s, averaged over response tokens (Hinton et al., 2015). The weight αu←vk ∈ [0 , 1] controls the distillation strength for this task and direction. 

Fixed-weight distillation. Under this objective, we consider two fixed-weight variants for com-parison with AMD. During Stage 2, F IXED -B I uses the same fixed distillation weight αBi for all participating tasks and both transfer directions, so each model receives supervision from its partner. At each evaluation point in Stage 2, F IXED -U NI selects the model with the higher validation score on each participating task as the teacher for that task. The teacher supervises its partner with the fixed weight αUni until the next evaluation. 3.2 ADAPTIVE -W EIGHT DISTILLATION 

AMD adjusts the distillation weight for each task and transfer direction as training progresses. Shared training probes evaluate candidate adjustments, and the receiving model’s task-specific validation scores guide the selection of each update. Figure 2 illustrates this process for one transfer direction. 

Global probing. At update round s, the vector αs contains the 2K task- and direction-specific distillation weights. AMD constructs three candidate configurations by decreasing, keeping, or increasing all entries of this vector. These actions are denoted by a ∈ {↓ , ◦, ↑} . Distillation weights are bounded mixing coefficients. We therefore apply increases and decreases in logit space and map the updated values back through the sigmoid function. This keeps the weights within the valid range and yields smaller changes near the boundaries. The logit-space step size is δs; the keep action leaves the weights unchanged. Each candidate is evaluated through a short training probe starting from the same current model pair. During each probe, both models train on shared batches using the candidate weights (Appendix A.2). Evaluation yields a validation score R(a)

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate each AMD branch against its sampling-matched SFT baseline, fixed-strength distillation variants, and prior task-balancing methods. Tables 1–3 report these comparisons across the evaluated backbones, together with the results of post-training model merging. 4.1 SETUP 

Experimental setting. We evaluated AMD on Qwen3-0.6B-Base (Yang et al., 2025), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Llama-3.2-1B (Meta AI, 2024). The training mixture covered mathematics, medicine, law, and general instruction following. It contained 100k examples from NuminaMath (AI-MO, 2025), 9,178 from MedQA (Jin et al., 2021), 50k from DISC-Law (Yue et al., 2023), and 10k from InfinityInstruct (Li et al., 2025a). The two AMD models shared this mixture. One used empirical sampling ( τ = 1 ), and the other used temperature-smoothed sampling ( τs = 3 ). We used full-parameter SFT with a learning rate of 

1 × 10 −5 for Qwen3-0.6B-Base and Llama-3.2-1B. For Llama-3.1-8B, we used LoRA with rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05, and the target modules set to all . The learning rate was 5 × 10 −5. In the main experiments, all models were trained for three epochs with an effective batch size of 64. Mutual distillation began at step 3000 with temperature T = 1 .0.

Distillation configurations. Across all three backbones, Fixed-Bi and Fixed-Uni used fixed dis-tillation weights of 0.4 and 0.2, respectively. Adaptive initialized all task- and direction-specific weights to 0.4 and updated them within [0 , 1] . Adaptive used a logit-space step size of δs = μsδ0,with δ0 = 0 .5. Tables 1–3 compare AMD with SFT baselines and prior task-balancing methods across three LLM backbones. On all three backbones, both Adaptive branches achieve higher macro-average scores than their corresponding SFT baselines. They also outperform all evaluated prior task-balancing methods. Relative to the corresponding SFT baselines, the gains range from 2.20 to 2.50 points for the empirical-sampling branch and from 1.57 to 2.54 points for the smoothed-sampling branch. Among the distillation variants, Adaptive produces the individual model with the highest macro-average score on each backbone. Its highest-scoring branch exceeds the best fixed-distillation branch by 0.26, 0.45, and 0.52 points on Qwen3-0.6B-Base, Llama-3.1-8B, and Llama-3.2-1B, respectively. The gains vary across sampling branches: on Qwen3-0.6B-Base, Adaptive and Fixed-Uni tie under smoothed sampling. Merging the two Adaptive models further improves macro-average performance. The merged models achieve the highest macro-average scores in all three main tables, reaching 43.75, 47.40, and 25.53 on Qwen3-0.6B-Base, Llama-3.1-8B, and Llama-3.2-1B, respectively. These scores exceed those of the better Adaptive branch by 0.30, 0.51, and 0.50 points, respectively. Across the three backbones, the merged models improve over multi-task SFT by an average of 2.91 points.

Online adaptation yields higher scores in both branches than keeping the learned final weights fixed throughout collaborative training. Following the same SFT warmup, Final-Weight MD keeps Adaptive’s final task- and direction-specific distillation weights fixed throughout Stage 2. It achieves 23.41 and 22.01 under empirical and smoothed sampling, respectively, compared with 25.03 and 24.54 for Adaptive (Table 4(b)). Updating distillation weights separately for each transfer direction yields higher scores in both branches than Direction-Tied MD. Direction-Tied MD retains task-specific adaptation but ties the two directional weights within each task. At each update, it averages the two branches’ task-specific validation scores for each candidate action to select a shared action for both directions. It achieves 24.59 under empirical sampling and 23.38 under smoothed sampling (Table 4(b)). Updating distillation weights separately for each task yields higher scores in both branches than Task-Tied MD. Task-Tied MD retains direction-specific adaptation but selects a single update action per direction and applies it to all tasks. It achieves 24.33 under empirical sampling and 23.69 under smoothed sampling (Table 4(b)). To assess whether a shared update action benefits both models under matched smoothed sampling, we compare Globally Tied MD with fixed-weight Same-temp MD. Globally Tied MD selects one action across tasks and transfer directions using a weighted average of task-wise validation scores. Its best branch reaches 23.41, compared with the best Same-temp MD branch at 23.18 (Table 4(a,b)). The shared update improves one branch but slightly lowers the other relative to Same-temp MD. # 6 CONCLUSION 

AMD addresses imbalanced multi-task LLM post-training by enabling models with different sampling distributions to learn from each other. Validation feedback guides the distillation strength for each task and transfer direction throughout training. Across three LLM backbones and six benchmarks, both trained models outperform their sampling-matched SFT baselines in macro-average performance. Optional parameter merging provides further gains and produces a single model for inference. The merged models achieve the highest macro-average scores among the compared methods, exceeding multi-task SFT by 2.70–3.24 points. Unless a method requires a different batch structure, we keep the data mixture, tokenizer, cutoff length, learning-rate schedule, task-utility mapping, and six-benchmark evaluation protocol fixed within each backbone family. Training runs are conducted on 8 ×A800-SXM4-80GB GPUs. The main Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen3-0.6B-Base runs use full-parameter SFT with sequence cutoff length 2048, bf16 training, cosine scheduling, warmup ratio 0.1, and learning rate 1e −5. The Llama-3.1-8B runs use LoRA rather than full-parameter updates due to the larger backbone size. The two-model AMD setup uses two task-sampling temperatures: τ = 1 preserves the empirical task mixture, while τ > 1 increases the relative sampling probability of smaller task categories. In the reported main runs, the two models use τ = 1 and τ = 3 . AMD enables cross-model distillation after the initial SFT phase; the main policy runs start distillation at step 3000 and use distillation temperature T = 1 .0. Method-specific policy settings are fixed before test evaluation and are read from the corresponding training configuration rather than tuned per reported benchmark. 14 Each probe uses the next batches that would be sampled along the current training trajectory. Both models share these batches, and all candidates use the same batch sequence. Model, optimizer, learning-rate scheduler, data iterator, and random number generator states are restored to their pre-probe values before each candidate and before training resumes with the selected weights. When validation scores tie, Adaptive leaves the corresponding distillation weight unchanged, while Fixed-Uni retains the current teacher direction. To keep the effective optimizer-update scale comparable, standard two-model runs use two models with per-device batch size 4 and gradient accumulation 2. MFTCoder and CoBa use per-device batch size 8 and gradient accumulation 1, while PCGrad and FAMO use task-grouped micro-batches so that each optimizer update covers all four task categories. This keeps the comparison data-matched, although not exactly wall-clock-cost matched. A.3 RATIONALE FOR SHARED PROBING AND TASK -WISE SELECTION 

AMD uses shared training probes to obtain task-specific feedback on candidate distillation-weight adjustments. Each probe starts from the same current model and optimizer states and applies an adjustment to the full weight vector. Its validation results measure each receiving model’s performance on each task after training under that candidate configuration. Selecting a single action from an aggregate score forces all tasks and transfer directions to share the same adjustment, even when their validation scores favor different candidates. AMD preserves these differences by using each task’s validation ranking to select the corresponding directional weight adjustment. The selected adjustments are combined into a new weight vector, and training resumes from the original state with these weights. This feedback-guided recombination enables task- and direction-specific updates while keeping the number of training probes per update fixed as the number of tasks grows. Because the combined vector can differ from every probed configuration, its performance need not match the individual probe scores. The probe results provide guidance for constructing the update, while the resulting training performance determines its effectiveness. For each transfer direction, Task-Tied MD computes the unweighted mean validation score across tasks for each candidate action. It selects the action with the highest mean score and applies it to all tasks in that direction.
