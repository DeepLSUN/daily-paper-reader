# TerMeZO: Ternary Sparse Zeroth-Order Optimization for Fine-tuning BitNet Models at the Edge 

## Houssem Sifaou, Prabodh Katti, Bipin Rajendran, and Osvaldo Simeone Institute for Intelligent Networked Systems, Northeastern University London, UK 

Abstract 

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) with first-order optimizers requires a memory several times larger than that required for inference. Memory-efficient zeroth-order optimization (MeZO) sidesteps this cost by estimating gradients from forward passes only. However, for BitNet architectures, a family of LLMs with ternary {− 1, 0, 1} weights and 8-bit activations, fine-tuning requires updating full-precision latent weights, and thus the memory footprint of MeZO no longer matches that of inference. A promising solution is to finetune only a subset of the latent weights, but existing sparse zeroth-order (ZO) methods either ignore the ternary structure or require first-order gradient information to build a sparse mask, which is at odds with the purpose of ZO fine-tuning. We propose TerMeZO, a sparse MeZO scheme that exploits the geometry of the ternary quantizer itself to identify the latent weights that are more likely to change values during fine-tuning, at no additional data or memory cost. Our convergence analysis shows that TerMeZO can converge faster than full-parameter MeZO, owing to its optimized reduction of the fine-tuning effective dimension. We run extensive experiments on BitNet models ranging from 1B to 3B parameters, spanning classification, instruction-following, and mathematical reasoning tasks. TerMeZO matches or exceeds the performance of full-parameter MeZO while substantially reducing the fine-tuning memory footprint. 

# 1 Introduction 

Deploying large language models (LLMs) on resource-constrained and edge hardware has become a practical necessity for a growing range of applications, yet it remains difficult, since the memory, compute, and energy footprint of modern LLMs far exceeds what such platforms can afford (Rajbhandari et al., 2020; Dettmers et al., 2022). Extreme quantization has emerged as the most promising route around this barrier. The BitNet family of models (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024, 2025) trains LLMs from scratch with ternary 

{− 1, 0, 1} weights and 8-bit activations, replacing most matrix multiplications with integer additions and shrinking the memory footprint enough to make LLM inference viable on commodity and edge devices. Adapting such a model to a new task or user requires fine-tuning, and here the memory advantages of quantization are lost. In fact, fine-tuning with first-order (FO) optimizers must store gradients, optimizer states, and activations, incurring a footprint several times larger than inference (Rajbhandari et al., 2020; Dettmers et al., 2022). Our work is part of the broader effort to relieve this bottleneck and to bring the cost of fine-tuning quantized LLMs back down toward the cost of inference. Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization (Malladi et al., 2023) offers a promising direction for this goal, as it estimates gradients from forward passes alone through simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (SPSA) (Spall, 1992). Being backpropagation (BP)-free, ZO fine-tuning avoids storing gradients and activa-tions, and for standard LLMs, the memory-efficient ZO optimizer (MeZO) (Malladi et al., 2023) fine-tunes at roughly the memory cost of inference. However, this parity does not carry over to BitNet models, which are fine-tuned through full-precision latent weights that are quantized on the fly at each forward pass, accu-mulating updates in full precision. As a result, even MeZO must perturb and update every latent weight at each step, so that its memory footprint no longer matches that of inference for the quantized model.

1arXiv:2609.33548v1 [cs.LG] 27 Sep 2026 As an alternative route to memory savings, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) (Hu et al., 2022; Houlsby et al., 2019; Lester et al., 2021), reduces the number of trainable parameters, while still relying on BP. Accordingly, PEFT retains much of the activation and gradient overhead that ZO is designed to avoid. Closer to our setting, several sparse ZO optimization methods build on MeZO to lighten fine-tuning further, yet none are designed for heavily quantized models like BitNet. FIM-S-MeZO (Guo et al., 2024) restricts fine-tuning to a subset of parameters selected by FO gradient information, a process that itself requires BP and thus partially negates the memory benefits of ZO. S-MeZO (Liu et al., 2024b) selects a sparse working set by weight magnitude, a criterion that ignores the ternary decision boundaries. QZO (Shang et al., 2026) avoids FO overhead by freezing the integer weights entirely and tuning only the full-precision quantization scales. QZO is effective when the required knowledge is already present in the pre-trained model, but too restrictive on harder tasks that demand acquiring new knowledge. In this work, we propose TerMeZO, a sparse MeZO scheme tailored to BitNet models that does not require FO gradients. Our key observation is that the ternary quantizer itself provides a natural criterion for identifying the latent weights most likely to benefit from fine-tuning. Weights closest to the quantization boundaries are the most sensitive to perturbations, and therefore the most likely to flip their integer value during ZO fine-tuning. In contrast, weights far from the quantization boundaries are effectively frozen in ternary space. TerMeZO restricts perturbations and updates to this sparse, boundary-adjacent subset of latent weights, while also updating the remaining full-precision parameters in BitNet models (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024, 2025), namely the embeddings, normalization layers, and language-modeling (LM) head. Unlike the selection rule in (Guo et al., 2024), which requires full-parameter FO gradients, TerMeZO’s selection rule requires only a linear scan of the latent weights. This scan can be performed offline, before the model is uploaded to the edge device, or on-device, provided the device has enough memory to hold the model together with its latent weights. We first show empirically that this boundary-proximity criterion selects the latent weights carrying the largest gradient signal, confirming that TerMeZO concentrates fine-tuning where it matters most. Then, we provide a convergence analysis proving that TerMeZO can converge faster than full-parameter MeZO by virtue of its reduced effective dimension (Section 4). The design and theory are validated through extensive experiments on BitNet models ranging from 1B to 3B parameters, including Microsoft’s BitNet-b1.58-2B-4T (Ma et al., 2025) and the Falcon-Edge family (Falcon-LLM Team, 2025). TerMeZO is seen to match full-parameter MeZO, exceeding it in several cases, while consistently outperforming existing sparse MeZO baselines that do not rely on FO gradient information (Section 6). The main contributions are as follows: • We propose TerMeZO, a sparse MeZO scheme tailored to BitNet models that enables ZO fine-tuning without sacrificing the memory benefits of quantization, making on-device adaptation at the edge more practical. • We provide a convergence analysis establishing that TerMeZO can converge faster than full-parameter MeZO owing to its reduced effective dimension. • We demonstrate across BitNet models from 1B to 3B parameters and a range of downstream tasks that TerMeZO matches or exceeds full-parameter MeZO at a reduced memory footprint. 

# 2 Background and Related Work 

Our work sits at the intersection of three lines of research, namely PEFT, ZO optimization for memory-efficient fine-tuning, and extreme quantization. We review these areas in the following, with additional details provided in Appendix A. 

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning. For modern LLMs, full fine-tuning via FO optimizers is increasingly impractical, especially for resource-constrained scenarios, requiring a memory footprint several times larger than that of inference to store optimizer states and activations (Rajbhandari et al., 2020; Dettmers et al., 2022). PEFT methods address this bottleneck by exploiting the observation that fine-tuning updates lie in a low-dimensional subspace of the full-parameter space (Aghajanyan et al., 2021; Lialin et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024). They freeze the pre-trained weights and introduce a small set of trainable parameters, achieving performance close to full-parameter fine-tuning. PEFT methods can be broadly grouped into four families, namely adapter-based methods (Houlsby et al., 2019; Pfeiffer et al., 2021; He et al., 2022; Mahabadi et al., 22021; Zhang et al., 2022), soft-prompt and prefix methods (Lester et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Li & Liang, 2021; Lester et al., 2021; He et al., 2022), low-rank reparameterization (Hu et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Kopiczko et al., 2024), and sparse fine-tuning (Guo et al., 2021; Zaken et al., 2022; Sung et al., 2021; Ansell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024a). 

Zeroth-Order Optimization and MeZO. ZO optimization estimates gradients from function evaluations alone, without requiring a backward pass. A widely adopted ZO estimator is SPSA (Spall, 1992), which approximates the gradient ∇F (w) of the objective function F (w) along a random direction z ∼ N (0, Id) as ̂

∇F (w) = F (w + ϵz) − F (w − ϵz)2ϵ z, (1) where ϵ is a small constant, requiring only two function evaluations per step. Other choices of the random direction z include uniform (Duchi et al., 2015) and Rademacher (Dang et al., 2026). ZO optimization has historically been applied to non-differentiable or black-box objectives, arising in settings such as adversarial attacks (Chen et al., 2017; Ilyas et al., 2018) and reinforcement learning (Salimans et al., 2017), where gradients are inherently unavailable. More recently, memory-efficient ZO (MeZO) (Malladi et al., 2023) demonstrated that SPSA can also be used to fine-tune LLMs using only the memory footprint of inference. MeZO’s success in fine-tuning hinges on the locally well-conditioned loss landscape of pre-trained LLMs near initialization, which bypasses the dimension dependence of standard ZO convergence bounds (Malladi et al., 2023). A growing body of follow-up work has sought to improve MeZO along several directions. In particular, reduced-variance methods (Zhao et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026; Dang et al., 2026; Dbouk et al., 2026) has been investigated in to enhance convergence. Other schemes optimize the perturbation using curvature-aware (Seung et al., 2025) or activation-guided (Lin et al., 2026) criteria, and gradient-informed perturbations (Mi et al., 2025). These methods are compatible with TerMeZO and they could be combined with it, although we do not explore this direction in this work. Information about curvature can be also leveraged to dynamically select which weights to update (Wang et al., 2026). However, such approach incur a memory requirement higher than full-parameter MeZO, and we therefore consider them poorly suited to the near-inference-memory of quantized models regime we target in this work. 

Fine-Tuning Quantized Models. Quantization replaces full-precision weights and activations with low-bit representations to reduce memory and compute. Post-training quantization (PTQ) methods such as GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), and SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) achieve 4-and 8-bit inference at a minor accuracy gap, while quantization-aware training (QAT) recovers the gap by simulating quantization during training (Nagel et al., 2021). Extreme quantization, 1-bit and ternary weights, has been studied for energy-constrained deployment. BinaryConnect (Courbariaux et al., 2015), XNOR-Net (Rastegari et al., 2016), and ternary weight networks (Li et al., 2016) demonstrated that aggressive weight quantization is feasible for vision models. More recently, the BitNet framework (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024) has introduced training LLMs from scratch with {− 1, 0, 1} weights and 8-bit activations, enabling matrix-multiplication-free inference kernels (Ma et al., 2024, 2025). To further increase the availability of BitNet models, recent works have also explored pipelines for distilling existing pretrained models into BitNet versions (Wu et al., 2025). BitNet distillation is orthogonal to our work, as it is typically applied prior to fine-tuning. The straight-through estimator (STE) (Bengio et al., 2013) is the standard BP surrogate in this regime, treating the non-differentiable quantizer as the identity in the backward pass. However, STE is known to be a biased gradient surrogate (Yin et al., 2019) and to interact poorly with optimizer states stored at low precision (Li et al., 2021). This further motivates the use of ZO for fine-tuning extremely quantized models. FIM-S-MeZO (Guo et al., 2024) combines ZO fine-tuning with sparsity, fine-tuning a tiny fraction of pa-rameters identified at pre-training, while keeping the remaining weights quantized to 4 bits. The work (Yang et al., 2024) reduces the effective ZO dimension by inserting adapters and adapting the per-step query bud-get to stabilize convergence. QuZO (Zhou et al., 2025) adopts a stochastic rounding scheme that lowers gradient bias for quantized weights, allowing fine-tuning with low precision forward passes. However, we found that this approach fails for ternary quantization (See Appendix H). Most directly related to our work, QZO (Shang et al., 2026) fine-tunes quantized models with MeZO by keeping the quantized weights fixed and fine-tuning only the quantization scales as full-precision parameters. We instead target complex tasks that may require fine-tuning of the ternary weights. Table 1 summarizes the related work for memory-constrained, BitNet-style deployment along five axes. 3FO PEFT methods (Hu et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2023) require storing full-precision adapters, gradients, and activations throughout training; evaluate matrix multiplication in full-precision; and do not benefit from sparse updates. Among ZO methods, sparse MeZO variants (Liu et al., 2024b; Guo et al., 2024) and TerMeZO save memory by restricting the perturbation and update to a sparse full-precision working set. TerMeZO stands out by obtaining this set directly from the ternary quantization, thus avoiding both the FO optimization steps required to construct a mask in (Guo et al., 2024) and the scale-only update constraints of QZO (Shang et al., 2026).

# 3 TerMeZO 

In this section, we propose a sparse version of MeZO tailored to BitNet models, a family of LLMs with ternary weights (Ma et al., 2024, 2025). BitNet models apply ternary quantization to most linear projections of the transformer blocks, while keeping the token embeddings, normalization modules, and the language modeling (LM) head in full precision (Ma et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Accordingly, ternarization is applied to every linear projection in the transformer blocks, i.e., the query, key, value, and output projections of the multi-head attention modules, as well as to all linear layers in the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) blocks. In the sequel, we refer to these layers as ternary linear projections . The proposed TerMeZO preserves the ternary arithmetic of BitNet in the ternary linear projections, perturbing a subset of the latent weights, which are quantized on-the-fly during the forward pass.

# 5 Memory-Footprint Analysis 

In this section, we elaborate on the memory savings afforded by TerMeZO over BP and full-parameter MeZO. We start by relating the memory footprints of full-parameter MeZO and TerMeZO for the same Bitnet model, and we then provide an experimental evaluation to support the analysis. The key difference between TerMeZO and full-parameter MeZO is that the former keeps a full-precision copy only of the active latent weights, while also additionally storing the sparsification mask. Let B be the number of bytes per full-precision weight, e.g., B = 2 for bfloat16, and let BQ be the effective number of bytes per packed ternary weight, e.g., BQ = 2 /8 for BitNet models (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025). Downgrading a frozen latent weight from full precision to ternary saves B − BQ bytes. A fraction 1 − ρ0 of the weights are frozen in TerMeZO, while each of the ρ0d active weights additionally retains its BQ-byte quantized copy. Hence, the peak TerMeZO memory footprint MTerMeZO can be expressed as a function of the memory footpring of MeZO MMeZO as 

MTerMeZO = MMeZO − ⌈ d (1 − ρ0) ( B − BQ)⌉ + ⌈ρ0d B Q⌉ + Mmask . (13) The mask size in (13) is given by Mmask = min (⌈d/ 8⌉, ⌈ρ0d⌈log 2 d⌉/8⌉) bytes, where the first term corre-sponds to the option of a dense bit-mask storing one bit per latent weight, and the second to an active-index list that records the ⌈log 2 d⌉-bit index of each active weight. # 6 Empirical Results 

We conduct experiments on a set of publicly available BitNet models across a range of tasks. Specifically, we evaluate on Microsoft’s BitNet-b1.58-2B-4T (Ma et al., 2025), a 2B-parameter model pre-trained on 4T tokens, and the Falcon-Edge family (Falcon-LLM Team, 2025), a set of BitNet models ranging from 1B to 3B parameters designed for on-device deployment. Through these experiments, we aim to answer the following questions: 1) Does TerMeZO match the performance of full-parameter MeZO on BitNet models? 2) How does TerMeZO compare to existing sparse and PEFT variants of MeZO on BitNet models? 

GLUE/SuperGLUE Classification Tasks. Here, we benchmark TerMeZO against the following base-lines: (i) standard BP fine-tuning with Adam optimizer; (ii) in-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020); 7(iii) full-parameter MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), which applies ZO updates to all latent weights and full-precision parameters; (iv) MeZO with LoRA (LoRA-MeZO); (v) FIM-S-MeZO (Guo et al., 2024); and (vi) QZO (Shang et al., 2026), a MeZO variant tailored to quantized models that perturbs the quantization scales rather than the latent weights themselves. Following the conventional MeZO evaluation protocol (Malladi et al., 2023), fine-tuning is cast as language modeling over a task-specific prompt template. We report results on six standard classification benchmarks from the GLUE (Wang et al., 2018) and SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2021) suites; namely SST2 (Socher et al., 2013), RTE (Dagan et al., 2005), CoPA (Roemmele et al., 2011), CB (CommitmentBank) (De Marneffe et al., 2019), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), and MultiRC (Khashabi et al., 2018). For all tasks, we report the accuracy on the validation set in Table 3 after fine-tuning using the BitNet-b1.58-2B-4T model. All ZO methods are run for the same number of optimization steps to ensure a fair comparison. Hyperparameters and experiment details can be found in Appendix G.1. We observe that TerMeZO is as effective as full-parameter MeZO fine-tuning on most of these tasks, and even exceeds it on some tasks. QZO, which fine-tunes only the scales of the ternary projections, is also observed to perform well. This is expected as these tasks are relatively simple, implying that the model already possesses the required knowledge and tuning only a subset of the full-precision parameters is sufficient. In contrast, LoRA-MeZO lags behind the other baselines. We attribute this to the known limitation of ZO optimization as a method for training from scratch (Malladi et al., 2023). In fact, unlike full fine-tuning, which starts from a well-conditioned pretrained initialization, LoRA introduces randomly initialized low-rank matrices. This effect is compounded in our setting, since, in order to remain compatible with the BitNet architecture, we constrain the LoRA projections to be ternary.

TerMeZO substantially improves over the base model and closely tracks full-parameter MeZO. QZO, by contrast, fails to match TerMeZO, indicating that for these tasks, fine-tuning the quantization scales alone is insufficient. We also observe a consistent performance gap between BP and TerMeZO for a given model size. However, TerMeZO’s lower memory footprint makes it possible to fine-tune larger models than BP would allow under the same memory budget. For example, the 3B model fine-tuned with TerMeZO outperforms the 1B model fine-tuned with BP, despite the latter requiring considerably more memory on a smaller model size as shown in Table 2. S-MeZO, whose mask selection relies on latent weight magnitude and ignores the quantizer geometry, falls behind by a significant margin. In contrast, while FIM-S-MeZO matches the performance of TerMeZO, it requires initial SGD steps on a separate dataset prior to fine-tuning, resulting in a memory footprint comparable to BP. This performance parity with FIM-S-MeZO further validates the effectiveness of TerMeZO’s selection criteria. Although FIM-S-MeZO performs similarly to TerMeZO in these experirmnts, it does so under stronger requirements (e.g., access to FO gradient information). We also observe that TerMeZO is more robust to the choice of learning rate. Specifically, for the same fraction of latent weights, some learning rates (e.g., 5 ×10 −4)cause FIM-S-MeZO to diverge, whereas TerMeZO remains stable (see Appendix H.1). We attribute this to the fact that a large proportion of the weights selected by FIM-S-MeZO are not reachable by perturbations, since they lie far from the quantization boundaries, which yields pure noise in the updates. Finally, we note that when an FO warmup before fine-tuning is affordable (e.g., offline in the cloud), combining the selection criteria of TerMeZO and FIM-S-MeZO could capture the benefits of both. We leave this investigation to future work. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Summary. This paper proposed TerMeZO, a sparse MeZO scheme for LLM fine-tuning tailored to BitNet models. The sparse mask selection exploits the ternary weight geometry and is inexpensive to evaluate for this class of models, requiring only a pass through all latent weights before fine-tuning. A convergence analysis of the proposed scheme is provided. Extensive experiments on a variety of datasets confirm the effectiveness of TerMeZO, showing that it recovers, and in some cases exceeds, the performance of full-parameter MeZO, while enabling a substantial reduction in the memory required for fine-tuning. By reducing the fraction of latent weights needed during fine-tuning, fine-tuning BitNet models can be carried out on edge devices with a near-inference memory footprint. 

Limitations. An efficient implementation of TerMeZO requires custom, hardware-dependent kernels to realize the memory savings in practice. Future work could target such kernels with a focus on both memory and latency. Additionally, due to the limited availability of larger open-source BitNet models, we have only evaluated our scheme on models ranging from 1B to 3B parameters. Validating the approach at larger scales is left for future work. 

Perspectives. A natural extension of this work is to evaluate TerMeZO on BitNet-equipped linear-attention or hybrid-attention models, which would allow us to jointly address the memory footprint of the KV cache alongside that of the latent weights. This is particularly relevant for RL-based fine-tuning where long generation rollouts can make KV cache storage a significant bottleneck. Another important direction is to validate the memory savings reported in this paper on real edge hardware with custom kernels, beyond the GPU-based setup used in our experiments.

## A.1 PEFT methods 

Full fine-tuning of modern LLMs with first-order optimizers is often prohibitively expensive, particularly in resource-constrained settings, due to the substantial memory required for optimizer states and activa-tions (Rajbhandari et al., 2020; Dettmers et al., 2022). Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods mitigate this cost by freezing the pre-trained model and optimizing only a small number of additional pa-rameters, motivated by the observation that fine-tuning updates typically lie in a low-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space (Aghajanyan et al., 2021; Lialin et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024). PEFT methods can be broadly classified into four families: 

Adapter-based methods. Adapters insert small trainable modules between frozen layers of the back-bone (Houlsby et al., 2019; Pfeiffer et al., 2021). Variants reduce the additional inference cost by parallelizing adapters with the residual stream (He et al., 2022), sharing them across layers (Mahabadi et al., 2021), or making them sparsely activated (Zhang et al., 2022). 

Soft-prompt and prefix methods. Prompt-tuning prepends a small set of trainable continuous tokens to the input embeddings (Lester et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022), while prefix-tuning extends this to every transformer layer by injecting trainable key-value pairs into the attention mechanism (Li & Liang, 2021). These methods are extremely parameter-efficient but are known to be sensitive to initialization and to underperform on smaller backbones (Lester et al., 2021; He et al., 2022). 

Low-rank reparameterization. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) freezes the pre-trained weights and learns a low-rank additive update, so that only a small number of parameters are trained while inference cost remains unchanged after merging. It is typically applied to attention and feed-forward layers and has become the standard PEFT method. Extensions include QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023), QA-LoRA (Xu et al., 2024b), AdaLoRA (Zhang et al., 2023), DoRA (Liu et al., 2024a), and VeRA (Kopiczko et al., 2024). 

Sparse parameter fine-tuning. A complementary line of work updates only a sparse subset of the original model weights, motivated by the lottery-ticket hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin, 2019) and by the observation that fine-tuning induces sparse changes (Guo et al., 2021). BitFit updates only bias terms (Zaken et al., 2022); Diff-Pruning learns a sparse difference vector with an L0 relaxation (Guo et al., 2021); FishMask selects parameters by Fisher information (Sung et al., 2021); and SAM/PaFi-style methods select coordinates by saliency or magnitude (Ansell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024a). Some recent works have also explored sparsity for ZO fine-tuning of LLMs. Existing sparse ZO fine-tuning methods select weights using criteria that either ignore the quantization structure or recover it only at high cost. S-MeZO (Liu et al., 2024b) picks weights by latent-weight magnitude, which is agnostic to the ternary decision boundaries, while FIM-S-MeZO (Guo et al., 2024) relies on first-order gradient information collected during pre-training to build its sparse mask. In practice, such information is often unavailable, necessitating first-order optimization on the target fine-tuning task or on a separate dataset as a prerequisite before the ZO fine-tuning process can even begin. 

## A.2 Zeroth-Order Optimization and MeZO 

ZO optimization has traditionally targeted non-differentiable or black-box objectives, as encountered in adversarial attacks (Chen et al., 2017; Ilyas et al., 2018) and reinforcement learning (Salimans et al., 2017), where analytical gradients are unavailable by construction. MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) showed that the same SPSA estimator can fine-tune LLMs at the memory cost of inference alone, and a substantial body of subsequent work has sought to improve upon it. Sparse MeZO (S-MeZO) (Liu et al., 2024b) restricts perturbations to a subset of weights selected by magnitude. MeZO-SVRG (Gautam et al., 2024) reduces variance via control variates. HiZOO incorporates diagonal Hessian estimates (Zhao et al., 2024). LoZO and its variants combine MeZO with low-rank perturbations (Chen et al., 2025). Block-coordinate variants update only a subset of layers per step (Zhang et al., 2024). Several recent works (Guo et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2026) extend MeZO to quantized models, which is the regime closest to ours and which we discuss next. More recently, ZO optimization has also proven effective for RL-based fine-tuning of LLMs (Qiu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). 15 A.3 ZO Fine-tuning for Quantized Models 

Severals efforts have explored fine-tuning quantized models using ZO optimization without needing STE. QuZO (Zhou et al., 2025) performs ZO fine-tuning entirely through low-precision forward passes, replacing the STE with optimized stochastic rounding to reduce gradient bias in settings with INT4 and INT8 weights. More recently, quantized evolution strategies (QES) (Xu et al., 2026) fine-tune quantized LLMs directly in the discrete parameter space via accumulated error feedback. QZO (Shang et al., 2026) proceeds by freezing the quantized weights entirely and fine-tuning the full-precision quantization scales solely. 27 Appendix G Experiments Details 

## G.1 GLUE/SuperGLUE Experiments 

Unless stated otherwise, the fraction of trainable latent weights is set to ρ0 = 0 .05 for TerMeZO and sparse MeZO variants. For all ZO methods, K = 5 Gaussian perturbation directions per ZO step are applied with a perturbation scale ϵ = 10 −3, and the learning rate follows a linear decay schedule. Following MeZO protocol (Malladi et al., 2023), fine-tuning is cast as language modeling, i.e., each example is rendered with a task-specific prompt template. We use the full training split of each task and report the test accuracy. The ICL baseline uses the same templates with 32 demonstrations sampled from the train split. All ZO baselines use the same batch size, learning rate grid, and step budget (Table 5). LoRA-MeZO attaches rank-8 adapters ( α = 16) to every ternary linear layer, freezes the base ternary weights, and ternary-quantizes the adapters in the forward pass so that the no-full-precision-multiplication property of the BitNet architecture is preserved. QZO freezes the ternary latent weights entirely and perturbs the per-channel quantization scales together with the remaining full-precision parameters. FIM-S-MeZO uses the same latent weight fraction ρ0 as TerMeZO, with the sparsity mask fixed a priori from a Fisher information estimate obtained by performing a small number of BP steps on the WikiText dataset (Merity et al., 2016).

## G.2 Instruction Following and Reasoning Experiments 

Datasets and protocol. For the supervised fine-tuning experiments, we use GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and Magicoder-Evol-Instruct-110K (Wei et al., 2023). Both are formatted with a single-turn in-struction/response template, 

Instruction: {instruction}\nResponse: {response} 

and the loss is computed on the response tokens only. Sequences are truncated to 2048 tokens. GSM8K ships no validation split, so its test split is used only for the final evaluation, and the validation set is carved from the train split. For MagiCoder, we train on a quarter of the train set and use 1% of the samples as the validation split. 

Hyperparameters. Table 6 reports the settings for both model sizes Falcon-E-1B and Falcon-E-3B. All ZO methods share the same batch size, learning rate grid, and step budget.

## H.2 Comparison with QuZO 

We include additional experiments comparing TerMeZO with QuZO (Zhou et al., 2025), which perturbs the quantized weights directly via stochastic rounding.
