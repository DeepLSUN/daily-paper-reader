Abstract 

Multilingual large language models (LLMs) have been shown to perform better on non-English classification tasks when the represent-ations of the given language are more aligned to English within the model. Several cross-lingual alignment (CLA) scores have been pro-posed for use with LLMs, along with multiple approaches for extracting embeddings from the models. We provide a comparative ana-lysis of 27 CLA score variants, examining how they differ and how well each predicts down-stream performance across three tasks. Cru-cially, while LLMs are widely used for gener-ative tasks such as machine translation, prior work has focused almost exclusively on clas-sification. We therefore investigate whether CLA scores are similarly predictive of transla-tion performance. To enable computing correl-ations across target languages, we propose a PMI-based translation metric, which is less de-pendent on the target language and correlates strongly with CHR F. We find that CLA with English predicts translation quality comparably to or better than source–target CLA, provid-ing new evidence that LLMs use English as an internal pivot language. 

1 Introduction 

Multilingual large language models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable multilingual capabilities (Singh et al., 2025) in tasks such as machine translation (Goyal et al., 2022), reading comprehension (Bandarkar et al., 2024), and text classification (Adelani et al., 2024), among others. While these capabilities have been extensively benchmarked, we have little un-derstanding of how multilinguality works. Cross-lingual alignment (CLA) scores can re-liably predict LLM performance on non-English downstream tasks without needing to explicitly run the tasks, as Ravisankar et al. (2026) and Kargaran et al. (2025) show. They specifically measure align-ment scores with English, based on the claim that LLMs implicitly translate prompts to English in their hidden states (Wendler et al., 2024). Align-ment scores can be obtained from embeddings (Kar-garan et al., 2025; Del and Fishel, 2022; Artetxe and Schwenk, 2019, inter alia) or tokenisation (Hämmerl et al., 2025; Limisiewicz et al., 2023). In this work, we comprehensively review ex-isting methods for obtaining sentence-embedding-based CLA scores, including sentence embed-ding extraction. We test the correlation between the scores and performance on three tasks: (1) SIB-200 (text categorisation; Adelani et al., 2024), (2) Belebele (reading comprehension; Bandarkar et al., 2024), and (3) Flores-200 & BOU-QuET (machine translation; NLLB Team et al., 2022; Andrews et al., 2025). CLA has been studied extensively on encoder-only models, but only a limited number of recent papers focus on decoder-only models (Hämmerl et al., 2025; Ravisankar et al., 2026; Kargaran et al., 2025), and none address the translation task, which remains understudied despite its popularity. Therefore, our work concentrates on translation. To our knowledge, no prior work has inspected the impact of CLA scores on translation perform-ance. We aim to answer an essential question: when translating from language src to tgt , which CLA is more predictive of quality: (1) src-tgt ,(2) src-en , or (3) en-tgt ? To answer this, we pro-pose a translation evaluation metric based on point-wise mutual information (PMI) which performs similarly to CHR F but depends less on the target language – a property crucial to our analysis. We analyse the contributions of CLA scores to prediction by finding optimal weights in an en-semble of the different alignment directions. Our findings suggest that CLA scores with English (src-en and en-tgt ) are generally more predict-ive, contributing new evidence for the claim that LLMs use English as a pivot language from a yet unexplored perspective. 1arXiv:2608.03446v1 [cs.CL] 4 Aug 2026 The main contributions of this work are: 1: A comprehensive review of existing methods for measuring CLA scores from sentence embed-dings. 2: A new multilingual fewshot method for extracting sentence embeddings from LLMs (Section 3.1). 3: Expanding the study of CLA in LLMs to machine translation (Section 6). 4: A new translation evaluation metric that is less target-lan-guage dependent (Section 6.1). 5: New evidence for the claim that LLMs use English as an internal pivot language. We publish our code on GitHub. 1

2 Background and Related Work 

Cross-lingual alignment. CLA, in the broad sense, is ‘the similar representation of similar mean-ings regardless of the language’ (Hämmerl et al., 2024). Previous work has used various methods of measuring this concept to estimate zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in encoder-only models (Conneau et al., 2020; Del and Fishel, 2022; Limisiewicz et al., 2023) and decoder-only performance based on alignment with English (Kargaran et al., 2025; Ravisankar et al., 2026). While we focus on the lat-ter, methods developed for encoder models can be adapted to decoders with minimal modifications. Embedding-based CLA has also been studied in encoder-decoder machine translation. Kudugunta et al. (2019) and Qu et al. (2025) used the similarit-ies of the embeddings to show that when the target language is known, the encoder will map the input sentence into that language’s space (as opposed to a language-independent space). Compared to that, the sentence embeddings obtained from an LLM are completely agnostic to the target language or the translation task as a whole, which considerably changes the predictive properties of the alignment. 

Obtaining text embeddings. Embedding-level alignment scores (described below) require a fixed-length vector representation for each sentence. 2 In contrast, LLMs assign a hidden state (embedding) to individual tokens. Various approaches for pool-ing token embeddings into a single vector exist; we refer to these as sentence representations .Taking the mean of the embeddings is a straight-forward option; however, Jiang et al. (2022) found that this approach with BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) performs worse than averaging static embeddings

Tokeniser-level alignment scores. Vocabulary overlap between languages has long been con-sidered a factor in cross-lingual transfer (e.g., Wu and Dredze, 2019). Rather than simply count-ing shared tokens, Limisiewicz et al. (2023) use the similarity of two languages’ token distributions to predict downstream transfer performance on cer-tain tasks. However, very few tokens are shared across script boundaries. Hämmerl et al. (2025) show that subword token 

alignability is more predictive of downstream trans-fer than literal subword token distribution over-lap. They define token alignability via the log-likelihood score of the Eflomal word aligner (Öst-ling and Tiedemann, 2016), averaged over all sen-tence pairs in a parallel corpus. The eflomal score acts as a proxy for how well the tokeniser supports learning shared semantics across languages, and operates quite differently from embedding-level alignment scores. 

English as a pivot language. Contemporary LLMs are trained predominantly on English data (OpenAI et al., 2024), yet perform well in other languages. Wendler et al. (2024) investigated this by inspecting the hidden layers of Llama 2 (Touv-ron et al., 2023), whose pre-training data is 89.7% English. They prompted the model to translate a word between two non-English languages, de-coded the intermediate hidden states, and found that the resulting tokens were often the English equivalent of the translated word, suggesting that the model operates internally in English even when prompted in another language. We provide ad-ditional evidence for this claim from a different perspective using novel methodology. Kargaran et al. (2025) and Ravisankar et al. (2026) build on this claim, showing that retrieval-based CLA between a given language and English correlates well with monolingual performance in that language on tasks such as reading comprehen-sion, language understanding, and narrative under-standing. Explicitly using a high-resource language, such as English, as a pivot has been a common prac-tice in machine translation. The techniques include translating into the pivot language as an intermedi-ate step, generating pivot-based synthetic data, or transferring a model trained on the pivot language (Kim et al., 2019). Evidence presented above sug-gests that this approach emerges in LLMs as well. We aim to further study this behaviour using CLA. manzana

Few-shot prompting. Few-shot prompting in-volves presenting an LLM with multiple solved examples of a task, followed by an unsolved in-stance. The model then follows the previous pattern and generates the answer for that instance. This ap-proach is typical for non-instruction-tuned models (Brown et al., 2020). In some of our experiments, 3we opt for a few-shot approach and exploit its ab-sence of an explicit instruction, since translating a prompt into all languages would be unfeasible, and using a shared English prompt would implicitly embed the alignment with English. We further expand the few-shot learning ap-proach to instruction-tuned LLMs, where we apply a chat template to the traditional few-shot prompt to simulate a conversation without an explicit in-struction. 3 Experimental Setup 

In our experiments, we use five methods to rep-resent sentences as fixed-length vectors and six methods to measure CLA scores from those rep-resentations, together with a tokeniser-level CLA metric, yielding 27 feasible combinations in total. We examine how these combinations correlate with performance on downstream tasks. We conduct our experiments with the fol-lowing models in their base and instruction-tuned versions: Qwen3-14B[-Base] (Yang et al., 2025), gemma-3-12b-{pt,it} (Gemma Team et al., 2025), and Ministral-3--14B-{Base,Instruct}-2512 (Liu et al., 2026). To ensure a fair comparison, we disable the chain-of-thought phase for all models. In all of the experiments, we work with 44 languages of various families, resource-richnesses, and writing systems, listed in Appendix A. Throughout, we use the Pearson correlation coefficient as the measure of correlation. 

3.1 Computing CLA Scores 

We compute all alignments on the 44-language sub-set of the Flores-200 -dev dataset (Goyal et al., 2022; 997 samples). For the embedding-level CLA scores, we use the sentence representation approaches described in Section 2: mean , p-wm 

(position-weighted mean; Muennighoff, 2022), last 

token, and English prompt (Jiang et al., 2024). Additionally, we propose a new embedding ex-traction method: fewshot , which guides the LLM to concentrate the meaning of a sentence into a single token. This is similar to the prompt 

method (Jiang et al., 2024), but fewshot relies neither on an English prompt – which carries im-plicit information about alignment with English – nor on translating the prompt into each language. The fewshot approach is inspired by DefSent 

(Tsukagoshi et al., 2021), who fine-tuned a BERT model to predict a masked word following its defin-ition. Then, they used the embeddings of the 

[MASK] token preceded by a sentence to embed. Unlike in DefSent, we do not fine-tune the model; we prompt it with a few-shot prompt to predict an entity from its description. We use the sentence to be embedded as the last description and extract embeddings from the token preceding the predicted entity. As the source of our few-shot data, for each language, we take the first five Wikidata entities (Vrandeˇ ci´ c and Krötzsch, 2014), sorted by QID, with descriptions of at least 10 characters. Listing 1 in Appendix B shows an example English prompt. Since all alignment scores are calculated per layer, we select the layer with the maximum align-ment score, following Kargaran et al. (2025). Next, we pair the sentence representation approaches with the CLA metrics described in Section 2: 

cos ine similarity, xSIM scores ( abs , dist , ratio ;Artetxe and Schwenk, 2019), Dali 3 (Ravisankar et al., 2026), and ANC (Del and Fishel, 2022). For tokeniser-level CLA scores, we follow Häm-merl et al. (2025) and use the Eflo mal score (Östling and Tiedemann, 2016) to estimate token alignability in the parallel corpora. Unlike Häm-merl et al. (2025), we do not estimate the priors on a larger dataset – due to data scarcity – and instead compute the score directly from Flores-200 -dev . Since a lower Eflo mal score indicates better alignability, we negate it to obtain positive expected correlations with task performance. 

Correlations within the alignment scores.

5 Belebele Benchmark 

Belebele (Bandarkar et al., 2024; 900 samples) is a multiple-choice reading comprehension dataset where the task is to select the correct answer from four options given a context and a question. Each part (context, question, options) is translated into each supported language, yielding a fully monolin-gual task in each language (unlike SIB-200). To avoid using English in the prompt, we use a few-shot prompting approach that shows three answered examples before the sample to be solved. The prompting template adds only newline char-acters to separate the parts of the text (or role switching in the case of instruction-tuned model). We do not introduce option numbering or field names (such as ‘Context:’). The LLM answers by explicitly stating one of the options, without sampling during generation. To save resources, we limit generated tokens to the smallest pre-fix that uniquely distinguishes each answer and check whether the generated prefix matches that of the correct answer. 6 Translation Benchmark 

Unlike the previous monolingual tasks, transla-tion involves two languages: src and tgt . A nat-ural question is whether the src-tgt CLA score predicts translation quality well. Alternatively, if LLMs use English as a pivot language, src-en 

and en-tgt CLA may correlate more strongly with translation quality. We primarily use the devtest split of Flores-200 for translation (1011 samples) and additionally the dev split of the BOUQuET dataset (Andrews et al., 2025) for out-of-domain validation. We con-sider all the possible pairs of the 44 languages in both directions. To avoid English prompts that might bias alignment measurements, we use a few-shot prompt of five examples, followed by the sen-tence to translate. An example is in Listing 5, Appendix B. We generate translations without sampling, limiting the number of tokens to 1.5 times the length of the reference. We evaluate translations using the CHR F score 4

(Popovi´ c, 2015) at the system level for each lan-guage pair. A limitation of CHR F is that its value depends strongly on the target language’s writing system, as characters carry different amounts of information across writing systems. This restricts us to measuring correlations within

7 CLA Metrics Evaluation 

The results across all three tasks allow us to make several recommendations regarding CLA metric choice. While there is no one consistently best-performing approach to measuring cross-lingual alignment, the position-weighted mean ( p-wm ;Muennighoff, 2022) sentence representation wins in more settings than any other representation. Sim-ilarly, there was no single preferred alignment scor-ing method, but average neuron-wise correlation (ANC ; Del and Fishel, 2022) wins in more settings than any other metric. Therefore, we suggest using p-wm in combin-ation with ANC in future experiments. However, in cases when sample-level alignment is needed, ANC is not applicable, and instead, we recommend using a retrieval-based metric (the simplest case is xSIM abs ; Artetxe and Schwenk, 2019). Compared to the embedding-level CLA scores, the tokeniser-level Eflo mal score (Hämmerl et al., 2025) achieves considerably worse correlation scores, except for the similar languages task. None-theless, in cases where the model’s hidden states are unavailable (such as in closed-weight models), it can serve as a rough estimate of the model’s expected performance. 

8 Conclusions 

We comprehensively review 27 approaches to meas-uring cross-lingual alignment from sentence em-beddings by combining five sentence representa-tion methods – one of which, fewshot , we newly propose – and six alignment scoring methods, to-gether with one tokeniser-level metric. Our newly proposed method outperforms existing methods in numerous setups, showing potential for further de-velopment. We work with 44 languages of varying linguistic properties and three LLM families. We assess how predictive CLA scores are for performance on three common NLP tasks: topic classification (SIB-200), reading comprehension (Belebele), and non-English-to-non-English ma-chine translation. For monolingual tasks, we show that the performance in the src language is well correlated with the src-en CLA score, which is consistent with the findings from previous work (Kargaran et al., 2025; Ravisankar et al., 2026). In the machine translation task, we address whether alignment between the source and target languages ( src-tgt ) is more predictive of trans-lation quality than alignment via an intermediate English step ( src-en and en-tgt ). First, we meas-ure translation quality using the CHR F score, which only allows us to analyse src-tgt and src-en . We find that src-en is generally more predictive of quality and only improves slightly by combining it with the src-tgt score. Next, to include the en-tgt score in our analysis, we propose point-wise mutual information (PMI) for translation—a proxy for CHR F—which we show to be less dependent on the target language. Using this method, we show that the src-tgt score does not further improve the predictions of the 

src-en and en-tgt combined. This contributes evidence to the claim that LLMs use English as a pivot language (Wendler et al., 2024) from a yet unstudied perspective. We summarise the predictive performance of dif-ferent CLA scores into three recommendations for best performance estimates: (1) Use p-wm +ANC 

for system-level tasks scoring. (2) Use p-wm +abs 

when sample-level scores are needed. (3) Use 

Eflo mal only when model weights are unavailable. 9Limitations 

Domain coverage. Highly multilingual parallel data remains scarce, and the Flores-200 dataset, while the best available resource for our purposes, covers a single domain. Consequently, our CLA scores and two downstream tasks share the same domain, which might inflate the observed correla-tions compared to what one would expect in more diverse settings. Controlling for domain effects would require multilingual parallel corpora of com-parable quality across multiple domains. Such a dataset (BOUQuET) exists for translation, which we use for validation. However, to our knowledge, there is no suitable alternative to the SIB-200 and Belebele dataset that covers a different domain with the same level of multilinguality. 

The few-shot sentence representation. Our proposed fewshot embedding method relies on Wikidata entity descriptions, which vary in quality and may not describe the same entities across all 44 languages. A more principled approach would use verified multilingual dictionary definitions, but no such resource exists with the language cover-age we require. Nonetheless, our proposed method remained the best-performing for certain setups, highlighting its potential. 

Target-language independence of PMI. While we show empirically that our proposed PMI metric clusters target languages less severely than CHR F, full independence cannot be formally established. The metric remains sensitive to model-specific cal-ibration, and the degree of residual dependence likely varies across models. We view this as an invitation for future work rather than a deficiency of the current proposal. 

Related languages experiment. Our side experi-ment with related languages (Section 6.4) covers only 7 pairs of related languages and is evaluated only on base models. Furthermore, token overlap between related languages may skew the PMI and 

> CHR

F distributions. We invite future work to exam-ine the role of English as a pivot language in more detail when translating between related languages. 

Language coverage. We selected the set of 44 languages we work with to cover various families, resource-richnesses, and writing systems. 17 of those languages (37%) are European, which is not representative of the global population. Nonethe-less, our experiments (Figure 5) show that the script and the resource-richness of the language affect the results more than the language’s geographical ori-gin.

C Target-Language Independence of PMI 

While the goal of the PMI translation metric is target-language independence, it is difficult to val-idate this property, as LLMs a priori perform bet-ter in some languages, making direct comparisons difficult. Nonetheless, we propose that a strong correlation within mixed target languages (such as that shown in Table 7, top; for experimental setup see Section 6.3) indicates target-language independence. Note that this is only a one-sided implication, as poor correlation can be attributed to various factors. Figure 5 shows the comparison in correlation between PMI (left) and CHR F (right). The plot for 

> CHR

F score – which is target-language-sensitive – contains clusters of target languages which do not follow the general pattern: most notably Chinese, for which the CHR F score is considerably smaller than a linear classifier would predict from the CLA scores. Such inconsistencies reduce correlation. While outlying target-language clusters are also present in the PMI plot, they are less pronounced, resulting in higher correlation scores. Furthermore, as Figures 7–9 show, the clusters are not consistent across models and seem to be nuances of the mod-els rather than the metric itself. Therefore, while we could not validate that our proposed PMI met-ric is target-language-independent, empirically, it is less dependent than CHR F and, unlike CHR F, does not exhibit systematic biases against specific languages across different models. 15 D Detailed Results
